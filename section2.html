Lesson 1

Stewardship, conflicts of interest and transparency

A manager looks after other people's money, so it must act in their interests, engage with the companies it owns, and manage or disclose any conflict.

The UK Stewardship Code

Published in 2010 (last updated 2024) by the FRC and FCA for institutional investors with voting rights in UK companies. It is voluntary, on a "comply or explain" basis: firms disclose on their website how they apply it, or why not. It expects investors to:

  • publicly disclose their stewardship policy and a robust conflicts policy
  • monitor investee companies, with clear guidelines on when and how to escalate
  • be willing to act collectively with other investors
  • have a clear voting policy, and report periodically on stewardship and voting

If dialogue fails, managers agree targets with the board and track progress, then escalate by collaborating with other shareholders or making a public statement before the AGM. Elsewhere, EFAMA adopted a stewardship code in 2018, and Japan's GPIF requires its external managers to sign Japan's code.

Conflicts of interest (FCA SYSC)

Firms must identify conflicts between the firm and a client, and between one client and another. They must keep an effective conflicts policy (with a description given to retail clients), maintain arrangements to stop conflicts harming clients, put information barriers around research produced for external use, keep records, and, only if a conflict can't be managed, disclose it. Conflicts commonly arise in execution, aggregation and allocation of orders, research, inducements and churning.

Front running (pre-positioning) means dealing ahead of a client's order to profit from its expected price impact. It can amount to insider dealing and is prohibited under the Market Abuse Regulation.

Transparency

Pre-tradeThe size and price of trading interest before a trade, such as firm quotes and the order book. It can range from the full book to just the best bid and offer.
Post-tradeThe price and volume of completed trades. Pre-trade transparency alone isn't enough, because many trades happen between or outside the quotes.
Check: a firm identifies a conflict it cannot prevent. What must it do?
Disclose the general or specific nature of the conflict to the client. Disclosure is the last resort, after trying to manage the conflict.
Lesson 2

Knowing the client: risk, liquidity, time horizon and tax

Everything in investment management starts with the client. Their circumstances, not the manager's preferences, set the strategy.

The findings are recorded in an investment policy statement (IPS): return objectives, risk tolerance, time horizon, liquidity needs, tax position and any constraints, together with the strategy to meet them.

Attitude to risk vs capacity for loss

Attitude to risk (ATR)Subjective: how much risk the client is willing to take (cautious, balanced or adventurous). Measured with questionnaires.
Capacity for loss (CFL)Objective: whether the client could keep a comfortable standard of living if the investments fell. Someone with less of their wealth at risk has a higher CFL.

If a client has a high ATR but a low CFL, the adviser must discuss the risks and may recommend less exposure. CFL drives the overall risk profile. Both change over time; moving to lower risk approaching retirement is lifestyling, and the pace of that shift is the glide path.

Other factors

  • Preferences and restrictions: exclusions (such as armaments), holdings that must not be sold, or themes (ESG, Islamic principles).
  • Liquidity: keep an accessible emergency reserve for known and unexpected needs (the book says enough to cover expected cash needs for three to five years). High liquidity needs mean lower-risk assets.
  • Time horizon: cash for money needed within about six months; bonds a little longer; equities generally only if the money isn't needed for five years or more; property for ten years or more.
  • Tax: residence, domicile, current and future income tax, allowances and tax wrappers. High-yield bond income held outside a wrapper hurts a higher-rate taxpayer. Transfers between spouses are generally free of CGT and IHT, so a spouse's unused allowances can be used.
Check: a client is enthusiastic about risk but has few other assets and relies on this portfolio. What decides the risk profile?
Their capacity for loss. The adviser should discuss the risks and may recommend reducing exposure.
Lesson 3

Suitability, the mandate and the suitability report

Every recommendation and every discretionary trade must suit the client, and retail clients must be told in writing why.

The mandate

A discretionary manager works under a mandate that sets the limited scope of its authority (the limits of authority). Changing the mandate requires a suitability assessment. Ongoing relationships require client information to be kept up to date. Under the Consumer Duty, firms must deliver good outcomes throughout.

What a firm needs to know

  1. The client's knowledge and experience of the relevant investments.
  2. Their financial situation, including the ability to bear losses.
  3. Their investment objectives and current risk profile.

Suitability applies to advisory and discretionary business, not execution-only. For professional clients, a firm may assume both per se and elective professionals have the necessary knowledge and experience. It may assume the ability to bear losses only for per se professionals (authorised firms), not elective ones (who opted in writing).

The suitability report

Required whenever advice or a personal recommendation is given to a retail client (and for life policies). It must set out the client's demands and needs, any disadvantages, and why the recommendation is suitable. Timing: for a life policy, before the contract is concluded; for a personal or stakeholder pension with cancellation rights, within 14 days; otherwise before the transaction. There are exceptions, such as a portfolio manager's recommendation on a regulated fund, or topping up an existing regular premium.

Costs and churning

Fees, dealing charges, spreads, wrapper and advice charges and commissions all reduce net returns. Churning is dealing excessively to generate commission. Trades that each look suitable can be unsuitable together if they are too frequent. Switching simply means selling one investment to buy another.

Check: may a firm assume an elective professional client can bear any investment losses?
No. That assumption is only allowed for per se professional clients. Knowledge and experience can be assumed for both.
Lesson 4

Diversification, monitoring and reporting

Combine assets that don't move together, keep checking progress against an appropriate benchmark, and keep the client informed.

Diversification

Correlation runs from +1 (moving in step, typically above +0.8 counts as high) to −1 (moving opposite, typically −0.8 to −1). Low or negative correlation cuts portfolio volatility without sacrificing return; that is the essence of diversification. A risky asset, such as an emerging market share, can even reduce portfolio risk if it behaves differently from the rest.

Reporting and compliance

Clients receive periodic reports in a durable medium. The book says the most appropriate method is current, near real-time information through a secure web portal, plus periodic meetings. Ratios such as Sharpe and Treynor may need explaining, or replacing with simpler measures. Monitoring checks progress against objectives, costs and performance, and keeps up with regulation.

Benchmarks and two key measures

  • Comparisons: a relevant market or specialist index; a universe of similar portfolios (a peer group); or a custom or composite benchmark for unusual or multi-asset mandates.
  • Holding period (total) return = (end value + income − start value) ÷ start value.
  • Maximum drawdown = the largest fall from a peak (the "high-water mark") to the following trough. It captures loss potential that volatility measures miss.
A portfolio rises to £200,000, falls to £150,000, then recovers to £220,000. Maximum drawdown = 50 ÷ 200 = 25%.
Check: two assets have a correlation of −0.9. What happens if you hold equal amounts of each?
Gains in one tend to be offset by losses in the other, so portfolio volatility falls sharply.
Lesson 5

The types of risk

Some risks belong to one company and can be diversified away. Some hit the whole market. Systemic risk threatens the system itself.

Unsystematic (specific, diversifiable)Business risk · industry risk · management risk · financial (gearing) risk: an all-equity firm has none · liquidity risk · counterparty risk: reduced by a central counterparty such as LCH, and highest in OTC markets · credit and default risk.
Systematic (market, undiversifiable)Default risk · inflation: erodes the real value of cash, as in 2022–23 · interest rate: being locked into 4% when rates hit 8% · currency · asset price volatility · commodity price volatility, driven by politics, seasons and supply · sustainability: an ESG event damaging a company's value.

Systemic risk

The risk of collapse of the whole financial system, where one institution's failure cascades through others, as in the 2007–08 crisis. In a panic, correlations between assets rise towards 1 as everything falls together, so diversification fails exactly when it is needed. It is almost impossible to insure against, because there may be no solvent counterparty. The central bank as lender of last resort is the backstop.

Common confusion: systematic risk is market-wide and can't be diversified away. Systemic risk is the system breaking down. A sharp fall in emerging market equities hurting a portfolio is systematic (workbook MCQ 8).

Investment horizon is also a risk: does the investor have the time and resources to see a long-term strategy through?

Check: why does diversification often fail in a financial crisis?
Correlations between assets rise sharply (towards 1) as they all fall together.
Lesson 6

Reducing risk: diversification, hedging, alpha and beta

Rather than buying only low-risk, low-return assets, a manager can hold riskier assets and then reduce the risk by diversifying or hedging.

Asset allocation

Spreading money across equities, bonds, cash, property and commodities, which respond differently to the economy (equities tend to do well in growth, bonds give stability in recessions). The right mix is unique to each client and should be reviewed regularly.

Hedging

Buying putsProtects against a fall but keeps the upside. Costs a premium.
Futures and forwardsLock in a price, removing the downside and the upside.

Hedges are rarely perfect and always cost something. Immunisation matches bond duration to the investment horizon. Active managers can reduce risk by hedging or selling ahead of weakness. Passive funds and ETFs reduce single-stock risk but follow the market down.

Alpha and beta

  • Alpha is the return above the benchmark. An alpha of 2.5 means the portfolio beat its benchmark by 2.5%. An index tracker should have an alpha near zero.
  • Beta measures systematic risk relative to the market. β = 1 moves with the market. β 1.5 is 50% more volatile. β 0.7 is 30% less volatile. β < 0 moves opposite to the market. β = 0 means no relationship.
  • Both are backward-looking and no guarantee of future behaviour.
A portfolio with β 1.5 when the market rises 8% would be expected to rise 1.5 × 8 = 12%.
Check: you want protection against a market fall but still want to benefit if it rises. Futures or put options?
Put options. They protect the downside while keeping the upside, at the cost of the premium. Futures remove the upside too.
Lesson 7

Where trades happen: exchanges, MTFs, OTFs, SIs and OTC

MiFID opened trading to competing venues. Each venue type offers a different balance of transparency, flexibility and risk.

MiFID (2007) requires firms to take all reasonable steps to achieve best execution, weighing price, cost, speed and certainty of execution and settlement. It promotes competition between venues. MiFID II (2018) recognises four venue types: regulated markets, MTFs, OTFs and SIs.

Regulated exchanges worth knowing

  • UK: LSE Main Market. SETS is the electronic order book for FTSE 100, 250 and Small Cap shares. SETSqx handles less liquid shares (auctions plus market makers). SEAQ handles fixed interest and some AIM shares. AIM, for smaller companies, is classed as an MTF.
  • Europe: Euronext, Deutsche Börse (over 90% of German trading on Xetra), SIX Swiss and Borsa Italiana.
  • US: NYSE (the largest by market cap, still with an open-outcry floor) and NASDAQ (most tech listings, higher trading volume).
  • Asia and the Middle East: Tokyo (prime, standard and growth segments); India's NSE and BSE (BSE has the most listed companies); Shanghai (A shares in renminbi, B shares in US dollars), Shenzhen and Beijing (SMEs, 2021); Hong Kong (Hang Seng: the 80 largest companies, with Stock Connect links); Saudi Arabia's Tadawul.

Alternative venues

MTFA multilateral system matching many buyers and sellers in shares, bonds and derivatives. Example: Turquoise (majority-owned by LSE Group). Regulated broadly like an exchange.
OTFIntroduced by MiFID II for bonds, structured products, emission allowances and derivatives. Equities are not allowed (equity derivatives are).
Systematic internaliserAn investment firm dealing on its own account, frequently and systematically, to fill client orders outside other venues. The same legal entity can't run both an OTF and an SI.

OTC trading happens directly between two parties. It is flexible and customisable, but carries more counterparty risk and less transparency. Global reforms are pushing OTC trades onto exchanges and central clearing to reduce systemic risk.

Check: can a company's shares be traded on an OTF?
No. OTFs can trade bonds, structured products, emission allowances and derivatives (including equity derivatives), but not equities.
Lesson 8

Algorithmic trading, VWAP and transaction cost analysis

Computers now execute much of the trading. The skill is choosing how to execute, and then proving it was done well.

High-frequency trading

Algorithms trade in microseconds to exploit tiny price differences (arbitrage) or to make markets (earning the bid-offer spread). Supporters say it narrows spreads and adds liquidity. Critics say it raises costs, destabilises markets and can create an illusion of liquidity. It was linked to the flash crash of 6 May 2010, when the Dow fell about 1,000 points (about 9%) in minutes.

The algo wheel

The workbook describes it as an automated system that selects the execution strategy (algorithm) for each order from pre-set options, and switches between them in real time as conditions change. For example, it might split a large order into smaller pieces when markets are volatile, or route to a dark pool when liquidity is thin. It removes human error and judgement, and gives consistent, transparent decisions that help prove best execution under MiFID II. The downsides are dependence on technology and poor handling of irrational markets.

VWAP

Volume weighted average price = Σ(price × volume) ÷ total volume. It is a benchmark for whether a trade was done at a good price: trading above VWAP may mean the stock is expensive relative to the day's average. It can cover any period but is best suited to intraday use.

Book example. 1,000 at $10, 1,500 at $12, 2,000 at $11 and 500 at $13 gives $56,500 ÷ 5,000 = $11.30.

Transaction cost analysis

TCA is the framework institutions use to achieve and evidence best execution. Pre-trade analysis chooses the execution strategy that minimises cost for an acceptable level of risk. Post-trade analysis measures slippage, the difference between the actual price and a benchmark such as VWAP. FX TCA is harder because FX data is incomplete.

Check: what does post-trade TCA usually measure?
Slippage: the difference between the price achieved and a benchmark price such as VWAP.
Lesson 9

Investing abroad: operational, currency and country risk

Overseas investing adds diversification, but also new things that can go wrong: different systems, time zones, currencies and governments.

Operational risk

Failures of internal processes, people or systems, so it is unsystematic. Internationally this includes system failures, fraud, political unrest, staff not understanding local rules, needing local authorisation or brokers, bans on short selling, time-zone delays between order, execution and settlement instructions, choosing a local custodian, different settlement penalties and buy-ins, safeguarding assets, and processing corporate actions.

Currency risk

The return in your home currency combines the asset's local return and the currency's return:

Rd = (1 + Rf) × (1 + Rfx) − 1
Book example. A UK investor converts £20,000 into $30,000 at 1.50 and buys shares that rise 25% to $37,500. The dollar weakens to 1.90, so the money converts back to £19,737. Currency return = 1.50 ÷ 1.90 − 1 = −21.05%. Rd = 1.25 × 0.7895 − 1 = −1.31%. A good share pick was wiped out by the currency.

It can be hedged with forwards, back-to-back loans, currency options, currency futures and currency swaps, but hedging is imperfect and costs money. It is generally recommended for overseas bonds (fixed income that depreciation would erode) and often avoided for diversified overseas equity portfolios, which are more volatile and spread across many currencies.

Country (sovereign credit) risk

Agencies look at the debt profile, banking stability, access to capital markets, GDP growth, political stability, national security, governance (rule of law, corruption), export profile, the balance of payments and fiscal policy. A downgrade means investors demand a higher yield.

Check: US shares rise 20% in dollars, but the dollar falls 10% against sterling. What is the sterling return?
1.20 × 0.90 − 1 = 8%.
Lesson 10

What changes a portfolio, and rebalancing

Clients, markets and products all change, so a portfolio needs regular review, and sometimes a complete rethink.

  • Client circumstances: losing income may force sales, possibly at a bad time, with penalties, or with early exits from derivatives at a loss. Then the portfolio needs rebalancing.
  • The financial environment: crises (2008, COVID, the cost-of-living crisis) change risk appetite. Some investors flee risk, others see bargains. Each cycle differs: today's profitable tech giants are not the loss-making dotcoms of 2000.
  • Rebalancing: after a significant change, reassess the whole asset allocation and set a new strategic allocation rather than patching the old one. Tax (for example assets outside a wrapper) may delay it.
  • New products and services: ETFs, CFDs, CDS, hedge funds and so on. Professionals must keep re-educating themselves.
  • Administrative changes: innovation and fee pressure from passive competitors are forcing back-office change.
  • Investment-related changes: rating changes (downgrades hit prices harder than upgrades help) and corporate actions.
  • Benchmark review: needed if the benchmark no longer matches the objective or risk, or holds assets the portfolio no longer owns. Agree the review frequency with the client.
  • Portfolio turnover costs: more trading means more costs and lower returns.
  • Time out of the market: for example when transferring between managers (sell everything and move cash, or transfer in specie while the old manager can't trade), or waiting for sale proceeds to settle before buying. Markets may move against you meanwhile.
  • Client churn: the rate at which clients leave the firm. Winning new clients costs far more than keeping existing ones, so firms should track it and find out why. (This is different from churning, which is excessive trading.)
Check: give an example of time out of the market becoming a high risk.
Moving a portfolio between managers: either it is sold to cash and the market rises before it is reinvested, or it is transferred in specie and can't be traded while markets fall.

Traps the wrong answers rely on

  • Systematic ≠ systemic. Market-wide falls are systematic risk. A collapse of the financial system is systemic risk.
  • Beta 1.2 means 20% more volatile than the market, not 20% outperformance (MCQ 4).
  • CFL, not ATR, drives the final risk profile when the two conflict.
  • Suitability doesn't apply to execution-only. The ability to bear losses can only be assumed for per se professionals.
  • Churning is excessive trading for commission. Client churn is clients leaving the firm.
  • Best client communication, per the book: a real-time web portal (MCQ 71).
  • CCPs reduce counterparty risk. Moving OTC trades onto exchanges reduces it too (MCQ 70).
  • VWAP is best used intraday (MCQ 61).
  • HFT's commonly quoted downside is the illusion of liquidity (MCQ 41).
  • OTFs can't trade equities. AIM is an MTF.
  • Hedge currency on overseas bonds; it is often not done for diversified overseas equity portfolios.
  • Puts keep the upside; futures and forwards give it up.
