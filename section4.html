Understand the concepts first

Section 4 is the biggest part of the exam. It covers the instruments themselves: shares, bonds, money market securities, hybrids, derivatives and swaps, and how to value each one. Each lesson starts with the core idea in one sentence, then explains it, works an example, and ends with self-checks you can tap to reveal. Allow about 4 hours.

Lesson 1

Shares: owning a slice of the company

Ordinary shareholders own the company and take the most risk: they are paid last, but they get everything left over.

Ordinary shares

Ordinary shares are the company's risk capital. If the company does well, shareholders share in the success through dividends and a rising share price. If it fails, they are paid only after everyone else, and can lose everything. Over the long run that risk has been well rewarded, but in the short run shares are the most volatile of the main asset classes.

Each share has a nominal (par) value, such as £1 or 25p. It matters only in two places: new shares cannot be issued below it, and it affects shareholders' rights in a liquidation. It has nothing to do with the market price. US "common stock" usually has no par value. Limited liability means shareholders can lose what they paid in but owe nothing more, except any unpaid amount on partly paid shares.

Non-voting ("A") sharesSame dividend, no vote. Usually trade at a discount to voting shares. Used by founders to keep control.
Deferred (founders') sharesExtra voting power, but the dividend is deferred for a set time or until a condition is met.
Redeemable ("B"/"C") sharesIssued to return capital to shareholders tax-efficiently, with a choice of redemption dates.

Preference shares

Preference shareholders come before ordinary shareholders for dividends and for repayment in a liquidation, but usually have no vote (unless their dividend is in arrears). The dividend is normally fixed as a percentage of nominal value: a 6% £1 preference share pays 6p. Most are cumulative: any missed dividends must be paid in full before ordinary shareholders get anything. Variants are participating (an extra share of profits when ordinary dividends are high), redeemable (with a date in the name) and convertible (they can be swapped into ordinary shares).

Shareholder rights

Ordinary shareholders can attend and vote at the AGM and EGMs (in person or by proxy, usually one vote per share), receive the annual report, vote on the proposed dividend, and have pre-emption rights: the right to be offered new shares before outsiders.

Ranking in liquidation (Insolvency Act 1986)

1Loans and bonds secured by a fixed charge
2Preferential creditors
3Loans and bonds secured by a floating charge
4Unsecured loans, bonds and other creditors
5Subordinated loan stock
6Preference shares (they get back nominal value if funds allow)
7Ordinary shares (whatever is left)
Check: what does "cumulative" mean on a preference share?
Any unpaid dividends from earlier years build up as arrears and must be paid in full before ordinary shareholders can receive a dividend.
Check: who is paid first in a liquidation, subordinated loan stock holders or preference shareholders?
Subordinated loan stock holders. Even subordinated debt ranks ahead of all shares.
Lesson 2

Valuing shares: earnings, dividends and assets

Every valuation ratio compares the share price with something the share gives you: its earnings, its dividends or its assets.

Earnings measures

EPS is profit belonging to ordinary shareholders (net income minus preference dividends) per ordinary share. Diluted EPS assumes every convertible, warrant and option turns into shares, so it is the worst case. EBIT is profit before interest and tax (operating profit). EBITDA also adds back depreciation and amortisation. It strips out financing, tax and accounting choices, which makes it useful for comparing companies internationally and in asset-heavy industries.

P/E ratio

P/E = share price ÷ EPS: how many years of current earnings you are paying for. A high P/E means investors expect strong growth, or the share is overvalued. A low P/E means low expected growth, or the share is undervalued. A P/E can also be high because profits have temporarily fallen or the company is a takeover target. Historic P/E uses last year's EPS; prospective (forward) P/E uses forecast EPS. The PEG ratio divides P/E by expected growth: below 1 looks attractive, above 1 unfavourable.

Dividend measures

Dividend yield = dividend per share ÷ price. A high yield can mean a strong cash-generating business (such as a utility, a "bond proxy"), or a share price that has fallen because the company is seen as risky. Dividend cover = EPS ÷ dividend per share: how many times profit could pay the dividend. 2× or more is prudent, below 1.5× is less sustainable, and below 1× means the dividend is being paid partly from past reserves (uncovered).

Gordon's growth model

A share is worth its future dividends discounted back to today. If the dividend grows at a steady rate g forever and investors require a return r, the price is P = D1 ÷ (r − g), where D1 is next year's dividend. If you are given the dividend just paid (D0), grow it first: D1 = D0 × (1 + g). With no growth the formula becomes simply D ÷ r. The book's example: a 10p dividend with no growth and an 11% required return gives a price of 91p.

Price to book

Price ÷ net asset value per share. Above 1 means the market values the company above its accounting net assets.

Check: a company has just paid a 20p dividend, expects 5% growth, and investors want 12%. What is the share worth?
D1 = 20 × 1.05 = 21p. P = 21 ÷ (0.12 − 0.05) = 300p.
Check: a share's P/E is 18 and earnings are expected to grow 12% a year. Is it attractive on the PEG measure?
PEG = 18 ÷ 12 = 1.5. That is above 1, so it is considered unfavourable.
Lesson 3

Depositary receipts and unlisted investments

A depositary receipt lets you own a foreign company's shares through a local, local-currency certificate. Unlisted investments trade liquidity for growth potential and tax breaks.

Depositary receipts

A bank holds the foreign shares and issues receipts against them. ADRs trade in the US in US dollars, with the depositary bank as the registered shareholder. ADRs have existed since 1927; BP, Shell and Vodafone have them. GDRs are issued outside the US, often by Asian and emerging-market companies, sometimes to build a presence in a new market as much as to raise money.

  • Sponsored programmes have one depositary appointed by the company, which gives the company control and lets it raise capital. Unsponsored programmes are set up by banks without the company's agreement and are now little used.
  • Holders' rights: dividends are converted into the DR currency after fees, and holders can usually vote through the depositary. They cannot take part directly in rights issues; the depositary sells the rights and passes on the cash.
  • Price ≈ underlying share price × DR ratio, adjusted for the exchange rate.
  • London GDR listing: freely transferable, at least £30m in total value, and at least 10% of the GDRs in public hands.

Venture capital and private equity

VCTA listed trust investing in small, higher-risk companies. It needs HMRC approval and pays no corporation tax on its gains.
EISTax relief for investing in new shares of a small unquoted company. The company needs gross assets up to £15m before and £16m after the issue, fewer than 250 staff, and less than 7 years' trading. Shares must be held at least 3 years, and the investor must not be connected to the company when subscribing.
SEISFor very early-stage companies: gross assets up to £350,000, fewer than 25 staff, less than 3 years' trading, and under £250,000 raised.

Private equity includes leveraged buyouts, management buyouts, distressed investing and mezzanine finance. Institutions like it for its low correlation with listed markets and historically high returns. It is high-risk, long-term and illiquid, so it must be well diversified. Unlike a bank, a private equity investor stays committed when a company struggles. It exits through a trade sale or a flotation.

Check: an ADR holder's company announces a rights issue. What happens?
The ADR holder can't take up the rights directly. The depositary bank sells the rights and distributes the proceeds.
Lesson 4

Corporate actions: changing the share capital

Some corporate actions just cut the same cake into more or fewer slices. Others bring in new money. The share price adjusts so that the value you hold stays the same.

Three types

MandatoryHappens automatically, such as a cash dividend.
Mandatory with optionsA default applies unless you choose otherwise, such as a rights issue.
VoluntaryYou must decide, such as accepting a takeover bid.

Dividends

Dividends can only come from realised profits; an unrealised gain such as a revalued building cannot be paid out. Interim dividends follow the half-year results. The final dividend is approved at the AGM. The key dates are: ex-dividend date (buy on or after it and you don't get the dividend; in the UK usually a Wednesday), record date (who is on the register, usually two business days later on a Friday) and payment date (aim: within 30 business days of the record date). In theory the price falls by the dividend on the ex-dividend date.

Scrip (stock) dividendThe company issues new shares instead of cash, so it keeps the cash. Shareholders pay no dealing costs but are diluted. The ratio is share price ÷ dividend: a 200p share with an 8p dividend gives 1 new share for every 25 held. It is taxed like the cash dividend.
DRIP (dividend reinvestment plan)The company pays the cash dividend and uses it to buy existing shares in the market for you, at bulk commission rates. No dilution, but it can take up to three weeks.

Bonus issues, splits and consolidations: same cake, different slices

No money changes hands, so the company is worth the same and the price adjusts to the number of shares.

  • Bonus (capitalisation/scrip) issue, 1-for-4: 4 shares become 5, so a 500p share becomes 500 × 4 ÷ 5 = 400p. Reserves are turned into share capital, and nominal value is unchanged.
  • Share split: each share is divided into several, each with a lower nominal value. Splitting 1 into 4 turns 800p into 200p. It makes the shares cheaper to trade.
  • Consolidation (reverse split): shares are combined, raising the price and the nominal value. 10 into 1 turns 8p into 80p. It is used when the price has fallen to or below nominal value, which would stop the company issuing new shares.

Rights issues: new money from existing shareholders

Existing shareholders are offered new shares in proportion to their holdings, at a discount. The new shares rank equally (pari passu) with the old. The price falls to the theoretical ex-rights price (TERP): the total value of old shares plus the new cash, divided by the new number of shares.

Book example. 1m shares at £4. 1-for-5 at £3.50. Value = £4m + 200,000 × £3.50 = £4.7m over 1.2m shares, so TERP = £3.92. The nil-paid right is worth TERP − subscription price = £0.42.

A shareholder can: take up the rights in full; sell the rights (they are renounceable); sell some to fund the rest ("tail-swallowing"); or do nothing, in which case the company sells the rights and pays over the proceeds.

Book error: section 10.6.4 says someone selling their rights "would receive £3.50 per right". That is the subscription price. A nil-paid right is worth the rights premium, about £0.42.

Open offers and new issues

  • Open offer: like a rights issue, but the entitlement cannot be sold, so it simply lapses if unused. That dilutes non-takers, so the listing rules cap the discount at 10%.
  • IPO methods: an offer for subscription means the company sells new shares directly to the public with a full prospectus, a national newspaper advert and underwriting. In an offer for sale, an issuing house buys the shares and sells them on to the public. A placing sells shares to selected institutions through a broker; with a lighter prospectus and no underwriting or advertising it is the cheapest method, and preferred for smaller markets.
Check: a 1-for-3 rights issue at 240p when the shares are 300p. What is the TERP?
(3 × 300 + 240) ÷ 4 = 1,140 ÷ 4 = 285p.
Check: why might a company with a 5p share price and 50p nominal value consolidate?
New shares cannot be issued below nominal value. Consolidating raises the price (and the nominal value), makes the shares more marketable, and prepares the way for a rights issue.
Lesson 5

Money markets: borrowing and lending for up to a year

Money market instruments are short, high-quality and liquid, so they are the nearest thing to cash with a return.

Maturities run up to a year, and three months or less is common. Issuers are highly creditworthy, so risk is low. Banks are the main investors. The return comes either as interest (yield-basis instruments) or as the gap between the discounted purchase price and par (discount-basis instruments, where in many countries the gain is taxed as income).

InstrumentIssuerKey features
Time depositBanksUp to a year, not negotiable, rate tied to SONIA, €STR or SOFR
Treasury billGovernmentIssued at a discount; the risk-free benchmark. UK: weekly auctions, usually 91 days
Certificate of depositBanksNegotiable fixed-rate deposit, up to 5 years
Commercial paperCompaniesUnsecured, issued at a discount, 8 to 365 days; yields more than T-bills
Bill of exchangeCompaniesA traded invoice. A bank guarantee turns it into a banker's acceptance
Check: how is the return on a T-bill paid?
As a capital gain. It is bought at a discount and repaid at par, with no coupon.
Lesson 6

How bonds work

A bond is a tradable loan. When interest rates rise, existing bonds with lower coupons become less attractive, so their prices fall.

The parts of a bond

  • Nominal value (par): £100 on UK bonds, $1,000 on US bonds. This is what is repaid at maturity.
  • Coupon: interest as a % of nominal, usually paid every six months on gilts. Coupons are paid gross and taxed as income.
  • Maturity: most bonds repay in one go (a "bullet"). UK classes are: DMO shorts under 7 years, mediums 7–15, longs over 15. The FT uses under 5, 5–15 and over 15.
  • Price: quoted per £100 nominal, above par (a premium) or below (a discount). The clean price excludes accrued interest; the dirty price is what you actually pay (clean + accrued).

Why prices and yields move in opposite directions

Suppose you hold a 5% bond and new bonds now pay 6%. Nobody will pay par for your 5% bond, so its price must fall until its overall return matches 6%. A bond's price is just its future cash flows discounted at today's rates: higher rates mean lower present values.

Early redemption features

CallableThe issuer can repay early, usually when rates fall, which leaves investors reinvesting at lower rates. Investors want a higher yield in return.
PutableThe investor can demand early repayment, which protects them. These bonds offer a lower yield.
Perpetual (undated)No maturity date. The coupon is paid forever unless the issuer chooses to repay.

Accrued interest

Coupons are paid to whoever holds the bond on the payment date, so a buyer pays the seller for the interest earned since the last coupon. Gilts use actual/actual: Nominal × (coupon ÷ payments per year) × (days accrued ÷ days in the coupon period). If a bond trades ex-interest, the buyer won't receive the next coupon, so the seller owes the buyer "rebate interest" and it is deducted from the clean price.

Check: why does a callable bond pay a higher yield than a similar non-callable bond?
The issuer will tend to call it when rates have fallen, forcing investors to reinvest at lower rates. The higher yield compensates for that reinvestment risk.
Lesson 7

Types of bond: who issues them and how they pay

The issuer sets the credit risk, and the coupon structure sets how the bond reacts to rates and inflation.

By issuer

  • Government (sovereign): the largest market. Often called "risk-free", but really minimal risk: governments do default (Argentina has done so nine times), and bonds in a foreign currency add the risk that the government can't get that currency. Issued through agencies such as the UK DMO, usually by auction.
  • Supranational: issued by bodies such as the IMF, World Bank, EIB and Asian Development Bank.
  • Agency: US government-sponsored agencies such as Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac and the Federal Home Loan Banks.
  • Local authority / municipal: US "munis" fund local projects and are often tax-advantaged for local residents.

By coupon type

Floating rate notesThe coupon resets every 1–6 months against SONIA, €STR or SOFR. Little interest-rate risk, so popular when rates are expected to rise.
Index-linkedCoupon and principal rise with inflation (RPI in the UK). The lag is 8 months for gilts issued before Sept 2005 and 3 months afterwards. Coupon paid = coupon × (lagged RPI ÷ base RPI).
Zero couponNo coupons. Bought at a deep discount; the whole return is the rise to par, and in many countries it is taxed as income.

Two points on index-linked bonds. With zero inflation the index ratio stays at 1, so you just receive the stated (real) coupon. Some markets have no deflation floor, so principal can be repaid below par if prices fall. Inflation protection isn't perfect: in 2021–23, index-linked prices still fell as investors expected sharp rate rises.

STRIPS

A conventional government bond can be stripped into each coupon and the principal, and each piece trades as a separate zero coupon bond. A 3-year gilt gives 7 strips (6 coupons + 1 principal). They let investors match a known future liability exactly, and they provide the capital-protection part of structured products. UK individuals pay income tax on the annual change in value, and strips can be held in an ISA.

Check: a 2% index-linked gilt pays semi-annually. Base RPI is 250 and the lagged RPI is 300. What is the next coupon per £100?
Half-year coupon is £1.00. Index ratio = 300 ÷ 250 = 1.2, so the payment is £1.20.
Lesson 8

Corporate debt: security, seniority and risk

The more security and seniority a corporate bond has, the safer it is and the lower the yield it pays.

Debt is usually cheaper than equity for a company: lenders take less risk (interest comes before dividends, and lenders are repaid first in a liquidation), and interest is tax-deductible. But interest must be paid whatever happens.

Secured debt

Fixed chargeOn specific, permanent assets such as a building. Ranks first.
Floating chargeOn changing assets such as stock and receivables, which the company can still trade. Ranks after fixed charges and preferential creditors.

A trustee watches over the deed and can appoint a receiver if terms are breached. Debenture means different things: in the UK it is secured on specific assets and must be registered at Companies House (unsecured debt there is "loan stock"). In the US a debenture is unsecured.

Unsecured debt, from most to least junior

  • Income bonds: the most junior bonds. Interest is only paid if the issuer earns a set level of income.
  • Subordinated bonds: repaid only after other creditors, so they pay higher interest.
  • High-yield (junk): rated below BBB/Baa3, with higher coupons for higher default risk.
  • Mini bonds: unlisted, sold to retail investors, held to maturity (3–5 years), illiquid, and not necessarily covered by the FSCS.
  • Speculative illiquid securities: hard to price and trade, aimed at specialist institutions.

PIBS are irredeemable, non-cumulative bonds from building societies that rank behind all other creditors. If the society demutualises they become perpetual subordinated bonds (PSBs).

Securitisation

Many small, illiquid loans (mortgages, credit card debt, receivables) are pooled, usually in an SPV, and bonds are issued backed by their cash flows. These are asset-backed securities; mortgage-backed securities are the biggest market. They turn illiquid assets into tradable ones and widen investors' choice. MBS were at the centre of the 2007–08 crisis, and rules are now stricter. The participants are the issuer, the underwriter (manages the issue), the debenture trustee (enforces the terms) and brokers/dealers (secondary trading).

Credit ratings

Investment grade is BBB−/Baa3 and above (Fitch, S&P / Moody's). Below that is high yield. A lower rating means investors demand a higher yield. The gap over a government bond is the credit spread.

Check: in the US, is a debenture secured?
No. In the US a debenture is unsecured and backed only by the issuer's creditworthiness. In the UK it is secured on specific assets.
Lesson 9

Bond yields, the yield curve and duration

Flat yield shows only income. Redemption yield adds the gain or loss to maturity. Duration tells you how much the price moves when rates change.

Yield measures

  • Flat yield = coupon ÷ clean price. It ignores any gain or loss to redemption, so it is most useful when the yield curve is flat.
  • Gross redemption yield (GRY) adds the annual gain or loss to par. Strictly it is the bond's IRR. A quick approximation: flat yield + [(100 − price) ÷ years] ÷ price. It assumes you hold to maturity and reinvest coupons at the same rate (the latter is reinvestment risk). Being gross, it suits non-taxpayers such as pension funds.
  • Net redemption yield (NRY) taxes the coupon but not the gain, so taxpayers prefer low-coupon bonds bought below par.
  • Real return: roughly nominal − inflation. More precisely, using Fisher: (1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1.
Book example. An 8% bond at 150.95 has a flat yield of 5.30% but a GRY of only 1.92%, because it loses about 51 points by maturity. A 3¾% bond at 112.41 yields 3.34% flat but 2.30% GRY, so it is the better overall return.

The yield curve

Normal (upward)Longer bonds yield more to compensate for tying up money for longer. Signals an expanding economy.
InvertedShort yields are above long yields. The market expects rates to fall, and it is a reliable warning of recession.
FlatSimilar yields across maturities. Signals uncertainty.

Two theories explain the shape. Liquidity preference says investors want extra yield for locking money away longer. Expectations theory says the curve reflects expected future rates.

Duration

Macaulay duration is the weighted average time until you receive the bond's cash flows, weighted by present value. A zero coupon bond's duration equals its maturity. Modified duration = Macaulay ÷ (1 + GRY), and it gives the approximate % price change for a 1% change in yield. Price sensitivity is higher when the coupon is lower, the maturity is longer and the yield is lower.

Book example. A 3-year 7% bond on an 8% GRY has a Macaulay duration of 2.81 years. Modified duration = 2.81 ÷ 1.08 = 2.60, so a 1% rise in yield cuts the price by about 2.6%.

Spreads and benchmarks

A corporate bond yielding 6% against a government bond on 4.2% has a spread of 180 basis points. New issues are priced off a benchmark: usually the government bond of matching maturity, but a benchmark curve where the bond has put or call features. Swap rates are also used. The swap spread (swap rate − government yield) is normally positive; it unusually inverted in 2010.

Check: which has the higher duration, a 2% 20-year bond or an 8% 5-year bond?
The 2% 20-year bond, because its coupon is lower and its maturity longer.
Lesson 10

Hybrids: convertibles, CoCos and mezzanine

A hybrid sits between debt and equity. It pays like a bond, but can share in the upside, or be forced to absorb losses, like a share.

Convertible bonds

The holder can keep the bond (coupons, then repayment) or convert it into a fixed number of shares. Because of that option, convertibles pay a lower coupon than ordinary bonds. They behave partly like bonds (sensitive to rates) and partly like shares, and are often subordinated.

  • Conversion ratio = nominal ÷ conversion price. £100 nominal with a conversion price of £4 gives 25 shares.
  • Conversion premium = bond price ÷ (ratio × share price) − 1. Book example: a bond at £120 converting into 18 shares at £5.75 has conversion value £103.50, a premium of 15.9%, so it is not worth converting.
  • A high premium means the convertible behaves like a bond. A low premium means it moves with the shares. A discount means converting is worthwhile.
  • For the issuer: cheap finance with tax-deductible interest, but it dilutes EPS and control. For the investor: upside if the company prospers, while ranking ahead of shareholders if it fails.
  • Rights or bonus issues lower the conversion price and raise the ratio, so holders aren't disadvantaged.

CoCos (contingent convertibles)

These convert automatically when a trigger is hit, typically when a bank's CET1 capital ratio falls below a threshold. That recapitalises the bank in a crisis, at the bondholders' expense.

Mezzanine finance

Mezzanine sits between senior debt and equity: usually unsecured debt or preferred stock, often with warrants or a conversion option (an "equity kicker"). Investors expect roughly 10–30% a year. Interest may be payment-in-kind (PIK), which rolls up into the principal, and principal is often only due at maturity. It is cheaper than equity for the issuer but costlier than secured debt, adds leverage and may dilute shareholders. It is used for buyouts, recapitalisations and expansion, and is common in private equity.

Check: a convertible trades at £112 and converts into 25 shares priced at £4.00. What is the conversion premium?
Conversion value = 25 × £4 = £100. Premium = 112 ÷ 100 − 1 = 12%.
Lesson 11

Derivatives: options, warrants, futures and forwards

An option gives a right. A future or forward is an obligation. All of them let you hedge or take a view without owning the asset.

Options

A call is the right to buy and a put is the right to sell, at the strike price. The buyer (holder, long) pays a premium and can lose only that. The seller (writer, short) takes the obligation. Intrinsic value is what you'd gain by exercising now; anything above that is time (extrinsic) value.

CallPut
In the moneyStrike below the priceStrike above the price
Out of the moneyStrike above the priceStrike below the price

Exercise styles: American (any day), European (expiry only), Bermudan (set dates, often monthly), Asian (pay-off based on the average price). Options are used for hedging, arbitrage, speculation and income (for example selling calls on shares you own).

Warrants

Like a call (or put) on a company's shares, but usually issued by the company itself and often attached to a bond to lower its coupon. Unlike a convertible, the bond stays in issue when the warrant is exercised. Holders get no dividends until they exercise.

Futures vs forwards

FuturesExchange-traded with standardised contract specifications (only the price is negotiated). Fungible, liquid, marked to market daily, and closed out with an opposite trade. Financial futures settle in cash.
ForwardsOTC and fully customisable (size, date, grade), usually with banks. Usually not marked to market daily. Less liquid, more counterparty risk, less regulation.

The Greeks

  • Delta: change in premium ÷ change in the underlying. Deep ITM ±1, ATM about ±0.5, far OTM 0. Calls are positive, puts negative. A short position flips the sign: being short an ATM put gives delta (−1) × (−0.5) = +0.5.
  • Gamma: how fast delta changes. Highest ATM near expiry. Long options have positive gamma.
  • Vega: sensitivity to a 1% change in implied volatility. Positive for long calls and puts, highest ATM.
  • Theta: time decay. Negative for long options and positive for short options, and it accelerates near expiry.

Volatility

Historic volatility is measurable but backward-looking. Future volatility is what you'd really like to know, but can't. Implied volatility is worked backwards from the option's market price using a pricing model. Black-Scholes prices European options only; the binomial model handles American options. A volatility smile means implied volatility is higher for deep ITM/OTM strikes; equity markets often show a "smirk" with higher implied volatility on OTM puts. The VIX is the 30-day implied volatility of S&P 500 options: the "fear gauge".

Check: a call with a 450p strike costs 42p and the share is 480p. What are its intrinsic and time values?
Intrinsic = 480 − 450 = 30p. Time value = 42 − 30 = 12p.
Lesson 12

Swaps: exchanging one stream of payments for another

A swap lets two parties trade cash flows, such as fixed for floating interest or an index return for interest, to reshape their risk without moving the underlying loan or asset.

Interest rate swaps

Two parties exchange interest payments in the same currency on a notional amount that is never exchanged. Terms run up to about 25 years. A company with a floating-rate loan that fears rising rates pays fixed and receives floating, so its cost is effectively fixed.

Vanilla (coupon) swapFixed for floating. The most common.
Basis swapFloating for floating, using two different reference rates.
SwaptionAn option, bought for a premium, to enter a swap later.

Quality spread differential

Two borrowers with different credit ratings pay different spreads in the fixed and floating markets. The difference between the two spreads is the QSD: the saving a swap can share between them if each borrows where it has the comparative advantage. For example: A pays 4.0% fixed or SONIA + 0.2%; B pays 5.5% fixed or SONIA + 0.7%. The QSD is (5.5 − 4.0) − (0.7 − 0.2) = 1.0%.

Currency and FX

Currency swapInterest in two currencies, with the principal exchanged at the start and end at a rate agreed upfront. That means more credit risk than an interest rate swap. Used for hedging and financing.
FX swapBuy a currency spot and sell it forward at the same time. Short-term, principal only. Used for liquidity and short-term hedging.
FX forwardAn exchange rate fixed today for settlement after T+2. The forward rate reflects the interest rate differential, so the market is used to speculate on changes in that differential.

Equity and other swaps

  • Equity forward: a single payment at maturity of an index's return against a fixed rate.
  • Equity swap: periodic payments of that kind. It gives index exposure without buying the shares, access to illiquid markets, and longer terms than exchange-traded derivatives. If the index falls, you pay both the interest and the loss.
  • Index swap: like an equity swap but on any index, such as bonds or commodities.
  • Volatility and variance swaps: a pure bet on volatility with no exposure to the asset's direction. Buy if you expect volatility to rise. The pay-off = notional × (realised − strike), using volatility or variance (volatility squared).
  • Bullet swap: settled in one payment at the end rather than periodically.
Check: what is exchanged in a currency swap that is not exchanged in an interest rate swap?
The principal, at the start and at the end.

Traps the wrong answers rely on

  • Gordon: using D0 instead of D1. If you're told the dividend "just paid", grow it first.
  • Price changes push ratios in opposite directions. If the share price falls, P/E falls and dividend yield rises (MCQ 39).
  • The two conversion formulas run in opposite directions. A convertible bond's ratio is shares received per £100. A convertible preference share's ratio is preference shares needed for one ordinary share. Read which way the question states it.
  • Rights vs open offer: rights can be sold (renounced); an open-offer entitlement cannot. The open offer discount is capped at 10%.
  • Placing is the cheapest way to float. An offer for subscription needs a full prospectus, advertising and underwriting.
  • Preference shareholders in a liquidation get back nominal value at most, after all creditors are paid.
  • Callable bonds pay more yield and putable bonds pay less. The yield follows whoever holds the option.
  • Duration goes up with a lower coupon, longer maturity and lower yield.
  • UK debentures are secured; US debentures are unsecured.
  • Index-linked lag: 8 months for gilts issued before Sept 2005, 3 months otherwise.
  • Warrants vs convertibles: exercising a warrant leaves the bond in issue; converting a convertible replaces it.
  • Currency swaps exchange principal at the start and end; interest rate swaps never do.
  • Commercial paper runs 8 to 365 days. Time deposits are not negotiable.
Workbook errors spotted in Chapter 4. (1) Section 10.6.4 says selling rights earns "£3.50 per right". That is the subscription price; a nil-paid right is worth the premium (£0.42 in that example). (2) The variance swap example gives a pay-off of $125,000, but $1m × (225 − 100) is $125m as printed. Learn the formula and don't rely on that example's figure. (3) Section 9.1.1 calls a floating-for-floating swap a "basic" swap; the syllabus term is basis swap.
