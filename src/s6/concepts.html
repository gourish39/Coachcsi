Understand the concepts first

Section 6 is the most mathematical part of the exam, but every formula comes from a simple idea. If you know what a number is trying to measure, you can rebuild the formula under pressure. Each lesson ends with self-checks you can tap to reveal. Allow about 3 hours.

Lesson 1

Data: where it comes from and how it's shown

Before you analyse data, know who collected it, what kind of numbers they are, and whether they cover everyone or only a sample.

Primary dataCollected for your specific purpose, so you know how it was gathered and its limits.
Secondary dataCollected by others, such as government agencies or international bodies, and published in a convenient form. It usually needs more analysis.
ContinuousCan take any value, such as a return of 4.37%.
DiscreteRestricted to set values such as whole numbers, for example the number of trades in a day.
CategoricalGroups rather than numbers, such as sector or meal choice. The mode is the useful average here.

Populations and samples

A population includes every member (all S&P 500 stocks). A sample is a subset, used when the population is too big to study. Larger samples are more representative. In a random sample every member has an equal chance of selection, which avoids bias. A non-random sample is picked to be more representative but can introduce bias.

Presenting data

A frequency distribution counts how often values fall into each band. A cumulative frequency table adds them up as you go. Choose band widths carefully, because they change what you can see.

ChartBest for
Bar chartComparing categories or frequencies
Pie chartShowing the parts of a whole
HistogramShowing a distribution. The area, not the height, of each bar shows frequency
Time seriesValues over time, such as a share price
ScattergramThe relationship between two variables
Semi-log graphRates of change, such as accelerating growth
Check: which chart would you use to see whether two funds' returns move together?
A scattergram (scatter diagram).
Lesson 2

Averages: mean, geometric mean, median and mode

Each average answers a slightly different question. For investment returns that compound, the geometric mean gives the true average growth rate.

  • Arithmetic mean: add up all the values and divide by how many there are. It uses every value, so outliers pull it around.
  • Geometric mean: multiply (1 + each return), take the n-th root, and subtract 1. It reflects compounding, so it is the right average for returns over time. It is always less than or equal to the arithmetic mean.
  • Median: the middle value once sorted. With an even count, average the two middle values. It is not affected by outliers, so use it for skewed data such as incomes or long-run returns.
  • Mode: the most frequent value. There may be none, or several (bimodal, multimodal). It is the only average that is always an actual observed value, and the useful one for categories.
Why the geometric mean matters. A fund gains 20% and then loses 20%. The arithmetic mean says 0%, but £100 → £120 → £96, a loss. The geometric mean is √(1.2 × 0.8) − 1 = −2.02% a year, which matches what really happened.

How skew moves the averages

Symmetrical (normal)Mean = median = mode.
Positive skewLong right tail. Mean > median > mode.
Negative skewLong left tail. Mode > median > mean.
Book error: section 2.1 calls the house prices £100k, £125k, £135k, £95k, £100k "bimodal". There is only one repeated value (£100k), so the data has a single mode.
Check: what is the median of 1, 3, 4, 5, 7, 9, 11, 12?
There are 8 values, so average the 4th and 5th: (5 + 7) ÷ 2 = 6.
Lesson 3

Spread and shape: how much returns vary

Dispersion measures how far results stray from the average. In investment that is risk, and standard deviation is the main measure.

From simple to thorough

  • Range = highest − lowest. Simple, but driven entirely by the two extremes.
  • Inter-quartile range = Q3 − Q1: the spread of the middle 50%. Q1 is the median of the lower half and Q3 the median of the upper half. It ignores outliers, but also ignores everything outside that middle half.
  • Variance = the average squared distance from the mean. Squaring stops positive and negative deviations cancelling out, but leaves the answer in squared units.
  • Standard deviation = √variance. It is back in the original units, which makes it the most widely used.

Population vs sample

For a population, divide the sum of squared deviations by n. For a sample, divide by n − 1. A sample tends to underestimate the true spread, and dividing by a slightly smaller number corrects for that (the "degrees of freedom" adjustment).

Worked example. Returns of 4, 6, 8, 10 and 12. Mean = 8. Squared deviations: 16 + 4 + 0 + 4 + 16 = 40. Population variance = 40 ÷ 5 = 8, so SD = 2.83. Sample variance = 40 ÷ 4 = 10, so SD = 3.16.

The normal distribution

A symmetrical bell curve. About 68.26% of results fall within 1 SD of the mean, 95.5% within 2 SDs, and 99.75% within 3 SDs. A fund averaging 6% with an SD of 4% should return between 2% and 10% in about two years out of three.

Skew and kurtosis: when reality isn't normal

SkewLopsidedness. Positive skew has a longer right tail (mean above median). Negative skew has a longer left tail (mean below median).
Kurtosis"Tailedness": how often extreme outcomes occur. Mesokurtic = 3 (normal). Leptokurtic > 3 has fat tails and more extreme events. Platykurtic < 3 has thin tails.

Real equity returns often have fatter tails than a normal distribution predicts, so extreme moves happen more often than the bell curve suggests. The book calls positive skew "leftward" while describing a long right tail; go by the tail and the mean-vs-median relationship.

Check: you have 12 months of returns from one fund. Do you divide by n or n − 1?
n − 1. Twelve months is a sample of the fund's possible returns, not the whole population.
Lesson 4

Correlation, covariance and regression

Covariance tells you whether two assets move together. Correlation puts that on a −1 to +1 scale so it can be compared. Regression measures how much one moves for each move in the other.

Correlation

A correlation of +1 means the assets move in perfect step, −1 means exactly opposite, and 0 means no relationship. The book's rule of thumb: 0.66–1 strong, 0.33–0.66 moderate, 0–0.33 weak or none. Diversification works best with assets whose correlation is low or close to zero. Correlations are not stable; they change over time, and history only gives an estimate.

Covariance vs correlation

Covariance shows the direction of co-movement in the original units, so its size is hard to interpret. Correlation standardises it by dividing by both standard deviations, which shows strength as well as direction.

From covariance to correlation
ρ = Cov ÷ (σA × σB)
From correlation to covariance
Cov = ρ × σA × σB

Regression

Regression fits a line showing how a dependent variable changes when an independent variable changes; for example a share's return against the market's. An upward slope is a positive relationship, downward is negative, flat means none. A steeper slope means a bigger effect. A scatter plot can show a pattern but cannot prove cause and effect. When a share's returns are regressed on the market's, the slope is its beta.

Check: two assets have standard deviations of 15% and 20% and a correlation of 0.4. What is their covariance?
0.4 × 15 × 20 = 120.
Lesson 5

Big data and artificial intelligence

Big data is too large, fast and varied for traditional tools. AI learns from it, but brings risks of bias and data security.

Big data is defined by the three Vs: volume (how much), velocity (how fast it arrives) and variety (how many forms). Client data is sensitive, so encryption, secure storage and GDPR compliance are essential.

StructuredSet fields in tables, such as daily closing prices. Easy to process.
UnstructuredNo set format: social media posts, PDFs, images. Hard to analyse, but useful for sentiment, as when one tweet moves a share price.
Semi-structuredSome tags or metadata, such as an analyst PDF with embedded EBITDA figures.
Multi-structuredA mix of all three, such as transactions, chat logs and app usage combined.

AI in finance

Uses include chatbots, customer relationship management, credit risk assessment and fraud detection. The main risk is biased algorithms producing unfair outcomes, which conflicts with the FCA's fair treatment of customers. Firms need ethical guidelines, monitoring and audits.

Robo-advisers build and automatically rebalance a diversified portfolio from an online questionnaire, sometimes with tax-loss harvesting. They suit low-cost, simple needs, not investors who need personal advice or complex strategies. Data itself is becoming a tradable commodity (the Shanghai Data Exchange).

Check: what type of big data is a collection of social media posts about a company?
Unstructured data.
Lesson 6

Technical analysis: reading the charts

Technical analysts don't ask what a share is worth. They ask where its price is likely to go next, based on patterns in past prices and volume.

It rests on three assumptions: the market discounts everything, prices move in trends, and history tends to repeat itself. (The weak form of the efficient markets hypothesis says this shouldn't work.)

Trends and movements

An uptrend makes higher highs and higher lows. A line along the lows is support; a line along the highs of a downtrend is resistance. Price movements come in three sizes: primary (years, such as a bull market), secondary (months, such as corrections or rallies) and tertiary (days or weeks, which is noise).

Chart types

  • Line: closing prices, often with moving averages (10, 50, 100, 200 days) to smooth them.
  • Point and figure: columns of Xs (rises) and Os (falls) of a set size, with no timescale.
  • Bar: high-low line with ticks for open (left) and close (right).
  • Candlestick: like a bar chart, but open-to-close is shown as a "body": clear or green if it closed up, filled or red if it closed down.

Overlays and oscillators

OverlaysDrawn on top of the price chart to give buy and sell signals, such as moving averages.
OscillatorsPlotted separately to show overbought or oversold conditions. The RSI (usually 14 days) runs from 0 to 100: above 70 is overbought, below 30 is oversold.

Patterns: channels (support and resistance) and breakouts, such as from a triangle. Relative strength charts confirm a breakout by comparing a share with the market. The head and shoulders is the best-known reversal (sell) signal, triggered when the price breaks below the neckline.

Check: is a moving average an overlay or an oscillator?
An overlay, because it is drawn on the price chart itself.
Lesson 7

Measuring returns when money comes and goes

Cash added to or taken from a portfolio is not performance, so it has to be taken out of the return. The two methods do this differently.

Holding period return

With no cash flows: HPR = (income + end value − start value) ÷ start value. To annualise, compound: 25% over six months is (1.25)² − 1 = 56.25% a year, not 50%.

Money-weighted return (MWRR)

This is the portfolio's IRR. The simplified exam version takes the gain after removing net new money, and divides by the starting value plus each cash flow weighted by how long it was invested:

MWRR ≈ (V1 − V0 − net inflows) ÷ (V0 + Σ cash flow × months remaining ÷ 12)

Money added early counts more because it was invested longer. The result depends on when and how much the client added, so it reflects the investor's experience. Use it when the manager controls the cash flows. It needs only start and end valuations.

Time-weighted return (TWRR)

Split the period at every cash flow, work out the return for each sub-period, then chain them together: (1 + R1)(1 + R2)… − 1. The size and timing of cash flows have no effect, so it measures the manager's skill. It is required by GIPS and more widely used, but needs a valuation every time cash moves.

Worked example. A portfolio starts at £50,000 and grows to £55,000 (+10%). The client adds £15,000, making £70,000, which grows to £77,000 (+10%). TWRR = 1.10 × 1.10 − 1 = 21%. The £15,000 doesn't distort the result.
Check: a manager can't control when clients add or withdraw money. Which return should be used to judge them?
TWRR, because it strips out the effect of cash flows the manager doesn't control.
Lesson 8

Indices, benchmarks and GIPS

A benchmark is only fair if it matches what the manager is trying to do, so you need to know how each index is built.

How indices are weighted

MethodHow it worksExample
Price-weightedEqual number of shares in each company, so high-priced shares dominateDow Jones Industrial Average
Market-cap weightedBigger companies count for more. Most major indices work this wayFTSE 100, S&P 500, Hang Seng
Equal-weightedEqual money in each share. Useful when giants dominate a cap-weighted indexMSCI USA Equal Weighted
Float-adjustedCounts only freely tradable shares (the free float)MSCI World
CappedLimits any single stock, sector or countryMSCI FM 15% Country Capped

An index starts at an arbitrary base value (such as 1,000). A divisor is adjusted whenever constituents or capital change, so the index doesn't jump. Bond indices set eligibility rules (currency, country, sector, rating, minimum maturity, issue size) and usually rebalance monthly. Equity indices rebalance quarterly or half-yearly.

Specialist indices

  • Yield index: tracks income return rather than total return.
  • Synthetic dividend (decrement) index: takes a total return index and deducts a predefined dividend, applied daily, such as the CAC 40 Synthetic Dividend Index.
  • Smart beta: a rules-based index built on factors such as low volatility, value or momentum (the active part), then tracked passively.
  • Composite (synthetic) benchmark: several asset-class indices blended to match a multi-asset portfolio, such as the MSCI PIMFA Private Investor indices.

Other benchmark types

AbsoluteBeat a minimum target, such as SONIA + 3%.
Manager universeThe median peer. It can't be known in advance and encourages herding.
Broad marketSimple and well known, but may not match the manager's style.
StyleLarge-cap, small-cap, value and so on, to match the manager.
Factor modelExplains returns through factors, but is complex and costly.
Returns-basedA blend of style indices that best explains the manager's returns.

GIPS

The Global Investment Performance Standards are not a benchmark. They are ethical standards for calculating and presenting performance, set up by the CFA Institute in 1999 (updated 2005 and 2020) and voluntary. To claim compliance, a firm must include all actual, discretionary, fee-paying portfolios in at least one composite (so it can't cherry-pick), follow set methods, make full disclosures, and use TWRR.

Check: in a price-weighted index, which company has the most influence?
The one with the highest share price, regardless of the company's size.
Lesson 9

Performance attribution: why did we beat (or miss) the benchmark?

Attribution splits the difference from the benchmark into the result of choosing how much to put in each asset class (allocation) and the result of choosing which investments to buy (selection).

Performance evaluation has three steps: measurement (what was the return?), attribution (where did it come from?) and appraisal (was it skill, the market or luck?). The difference between portfolio and benchmark return is the active return.

Worked example. The benchmark is 70% equities (returned 8%) and 30% bonds (returned 2%), so benchmark return = 0.7×8 + 0.3×2 = 6.2%.
The fund held 50/50. Using the benchmark's returns, that mix would have earned 0.5×8 + 0.5×2 = 5.0%. So asset allocation cost 5.0 − 6.2 = −1.2% (it was underweight the better-performing asset).
The fund's own equities returned 10% and its bonds 3%: actual = 0.5×10 + 0.5×3 = 6.5%. So stock selection added 6.5 − 5.0 = +1.5%.
Total active return = 6.5 − 6.2 = +0.3%, which equals −1.2 + 1.5.

The book's approach also works in money: apply each set of weights and returns to the starting fund value and compare the end values.

Check: a fund overweights the asset class that did best, but picks weak shares within it. Which effect is positive?
Asset allocation is positive. Stock selection is negative.

Risk-adjusted returns and multi-factor models

A higher return means little if more risk was taken to get it. Risk-adjusted ratios divide the reward by the risk, and differ only in how they measure risk.

Worked example. A fund returns 11% with an SD of 16% and a beta of 0.8. The risk-free rate is 3% and the market returned 9%. Sharpe = 8 ÷ 16 = 0.5. Treynor = 8 ÷ 0.8 = 10. CAPM expected = 3 + 0.8 × 6 = 7.8%, so Jensen's alpha = +3.2%.
Book error: the Sharpe example in section 9.1 shows the answer as "1%". Sharpe is a ratio, so (12 − 2) ÷ 10 = 1.0.

Multi-factor models

CAPM uses one factor (beta). Multi-factor models use several, each with its own sensitivity:

MacroeconomicInflation, interest rates, industrial production. APT is the classic example.
FundamentalCompany attributes such as size, book-to-price and P/E. Fama-French is the example.
StatisticalFactors found from historical return patterns. The book puts Carhart (which adds momentum) here.

Research highlights four factors: unanticipated inflation, changes in industrial production, changes in the bond default premium and shifts in the yield curve. These models explain returns better than CAPM with fewer assumptions, but rely on the factors being uncorrelated and their relationships staying stable.

Check: you want to compare two well-diversified pension funds. Sharpe or Treynor?
Treynor. In a well-diversified fund only systematic risk (beta) remains, so that is the relevant risk measure.
