Lesson 1

Fund basics: open-ended, closed-ended and legal forms

A collective pools many investors' money into one diversified portfolio. Whether it is open-ended or closed-ended decides how its price is set.

Open-endedCreates units when investors buy and cancels them when they sell, so it is always priced at NAV per unit. Dealt through the manager. Examples: unit trusts, OEICs and (via the exchange) ETFs.
Closed-endedA fixed number of shares, issued at an IPO and then traded on an exchange. The price is set by supply and demand, so it can sit at a premium or discount to NAV. Example: investment trusts.
NAV per unit = (assets − liabilities) ÷ units. £125m of investments and 100m units gives a NAV of £1.25.

A fund mandate sets out the aim, strategy, regions and sectors, allocation limits, permitted securities, whether the fund can short, hedge or gear, its benchmark and the maximum tracking error. A factsheet summarises it.

Three legal forms

CorporateA company with variable capital (ICVC). Investors are shareholders. OEIC, SICAV, US mutual fund.
TrustCreated by a deed between the manager and the trustee. Investors are beneficiaries owning units. UK unit trust; also Singapore and New Zealand.
ContractualThe investor contracts with a management company and owns a proportional share. Germany, Denmark, Portugal, Sweden, Switzerland. In Luxembourg, the FCP is the contractual form and the SICAV the corporate one.

Authorised funds are approved by the regulator, diversified and can be marketed to the public. Unauthorised funds are legal but restricted in who they can be marketed to. IOSCO principles for collectives cover independent review and oversight, a legal framework (fiduciary duty, disclosure, conflicts), investor rights (including the right to exit), internal controls, transparency, and industry codes of conduct.

Check: why can an investment trust trade at a discount but a unit trust can't?
A unit trust creates and cancels units at NAV. An investment trust has a fixed number of shares traded on the market, so its price depends on supply and demand.
Lesson 2

Unit trusts: structure, pricing and charges

A unit trust is a trust: a trustee holds the assets for the investors, and a manager invests them.

Types and tax

  • Authorised unit trust (AUT): constituted by a trust deed between manager and trustee. It pays no tax on income or gains within the fund; investors are taxed on what they receive and on disposal. It can be UCITS-certified for marketing across the EU.
  • Unauthorised unit trust (UUT): not marketed to the public, and taxed within the fund.
  • Exempt unit trust (EUT): no CGT within the fund, so only for CGT-exempt holders: pensions and charities.
  • Income units pay out distributions; accumulation units reinvest them (still taxable outside a wrapper). If more than 60% is in fixed income, distributions are paid as interest; otherwise as dividends.

The manager markets the trust, manages it under the deed, keeps the unit register for the trustee, and reports breaches to the regulator.

Pricing

Valued daily at a valuation point using NAV. Most unit trusts are dual priced: a higher offer (buy) price and a lower bid (sell) price, with a spread of about 2–3%.

Offer basisA rising market with mostly buyers. The spread is moved to the top of its permitted range, so buyers pay the maximum.
Bid basisA falling market with mostly sellers. The spread is moved to the bottom, so sellers receive the minimum.

Dual pricing makes those who cause dealing costs pay them. With single pricing, the fund absorbs those costs and all investors suffer dilution.

Historic pricingYou pay the price from the last valuation point. Known in advance, but may be stale.
Forward pricingYou pay the price at the next valuation point. Unknown in advance, but more accurate.

The FCA requires a historic-pricing manager to move to forward pricing if it believes the fund's value has moved by 2% or more since the last valuation, and when an investor asks for forward pricing.

Charges

Initial charge (added to the buying price; often lower or nil now), annual management charge (about 0.5–1.5%, higher for active funds), performance fee (usually only on new highs above a high-water mark), and exit charge (on a sliding scale, common when there is no initial charge). The OCF excludes performance fees; the TER includes them. Neither includes one-off entry or exit charges. RDR, MiFID II, PRIIPs and the Consumer Duty all push for transparent, fair-value charging.

Check: markets are falling and most unit trust investors are selling. Which pricing basis will the manager probably use?
The bid basis: the spread moves to the bottom of its range.
Lesson 3

OEICs, money market funds and manager-of-managers

An OEIC is the company version of a unit trust: open-ended, priced at NAV, but structured as a company with shares.

  • Also called an ICVC (or SICAV in Europe). Can be an umbrella with several sub-funds, each with its own assets and register. Has income and accumulation share classes, with the same 60% fixed-income rule as unit trusts.
  • It must have an authorised corporate director (ACD) for day-to-day management, and an independent depositary (usually a bank) that holds legal title to the assets, keeps them safe and checks compliance.
  • Single priced (no bid-offer spread), with charges shown separately. Usually historic pricing. It cannot trade at a discount to NAV. The ACD may "run a box" of shares to reissue rather than create and cancel them.
  • Can be UCITS, a non-UCITS retail scheme (NURS) or a qualified investor scheme. Governed by the FCA's COLL rules. Like unit trusts, OEICs generally can't borrow to invest.

Money market funds

They pool money into many highly rated short-term instruments. They usually pay more than a bank's money market account and spread the risk across many issuers, but their value can still fall. The IA has two sectors: short-term MMFs, which keep a constant NAV, and standard MMFs, with a fluctuating NAV.

Manager of managers

The fund appoints several specialist managers, each managing their own part of the portfolio, and monitors them against benchmarks. It diversifies away the risk of any one manager doing badly. (A fund of funds does something similar by buying other funds.)

Check: who holds legal title to an OEIC's investments?
The depositary, which may appoint sub-custodians but remains responsible.
Lesson 4

Exchange-traded products: ETFs, ETCs and ETNs

ETPs trade on an exchange like shares, but what sits behind them differs: a fund, a collateralised commodity note, or a bank's unsecured promise.

ETFs

Usually track an index and trade close to NAV, with real-time pricing, transparent holdings, low costs and no initial or exit charges (you pay normal broking commission). In Europe they are usually UCITS funds. Replication is physical (full or sampled) or synthetic (swaps). Drawbacks: tracking error from cash drag and costs, no risk management, and they follow the market down.

Reverse (inverse) ETFsRise when the index falls, so you can effectively go short without borrowing stock.
Leveraged ETFsAim for 2× or 3× (or −2×/−3×) the index's daily return. Over longer periods results can differ a lot from the multiple, especially in volatile markets. Higher charges.

ETCs and ETNs

ETC (exchange-traded commodity)Usually tracks one commodity or commodity index, mostly by rolling futures (which adds roll costs). Not UCITS, but fully collateralised, so counterparty risk is effectively hedged.
ETN (exchange-traded note)A senior, unsecured, unsubordinated debt security of a bank, which promises the index return minus fees at maturity. It owns nothing, pays no interest and has no votes. You bear the bank's credit risk. Can be collateralised or uncollateralised.

ETP risks

  • Stock lending brings counterparty and collateral risk, managed with legal agreements, over-collateralisation and daily mark-to-market.
  • Swap counterparty risk in synthetic ETFs. UCITS limits: no more than 10% of NAV exposed to any one swap counterparty, and no more than 20% in instruments of one issuer.
  • Liquidity risk where the underlying assets are illiquid (small caps, low-rated bonds, property).

Charges are low, and there is no stamp duty on UK purchases of ETPs, which are typically domiciled offshore.

Check: an investor holds an ETN and the issuing bank fails. What is the risk?
A large or total loss. An ETN is unsecured bank debt and owns none of the assets it tracks.
Lesson 5

Investment trusts: closed-ended companies

An investment trust is a listed company that invests in other assets. Its share price floats free of its NAV.

  • It has a board of directors, which usually outsources investment management.
  • Closed-ended: a fixed number of shares, traded on the exchange. Some offer periodic redemptions.
  • Price vs NAV: at a premium (above NAV) or discount (below). Usually a discount, reflecting the market's view of the trust's quality and investments. The discount narrows (or turns into a premium) when the trust is about to be wound up or is involved in a merger or takeover.
  • Can borrow (gear) within limits, which adds return and risk. Open-ended funds generally can't.
  • Taxed like ordinary shares.
NAV 320p, share price 288p: discount = (320 − 288) ÷ 320 = 10%. NAV 150p, price 159p: premium = 9 ÷ 150 = 6%.

Closed-ended funds are also better at holding illiquid assets (property, infrastructure): sellers sell to other investors, so the manager never has to dump assets to meet redemptions.

Check: an investment trust announces it will be wound up next year. What usually happens to its discount?
It narrows, because investors expect to receive close to NAV when the assets are realised.
Lesson 6

Choosing a fund

Look past headline performance: check what the fund is trying to do, whether it can keep doing it, what it costs and how it is taxed.

  • Objectives, mandate and strategy must match the client, including active vs passive and any ESG requirements.
  • Fund size: a big active fund may struggle, especially in small caps (it buys too large a share of each company, moves prices, or has to drift up the cap scale). Size matters little for passive or large-cap funds, and larger funds spread costs.
  • Track record: judge it against the right benchmark and the market conditions, since styles go in and out of favour. Watch for closet trackers (active fees for index-like holdings) and survivorship bias (failed funds drop out of the figures, so averages look better). For passive funds check tracking error: below about 2% a year is low.
  • Fund ratings: past performance may be chance, and managers change. Ratings are filters, not predictions.
  • Manager ratings: often use the information ratio. Above 1 is unusual and impressive, 0.5 is still impressive, and negative means underperformance.
  • Key person risk: if a star manager leaves, redemptions may follow. The impact is smaller with a diverse team and a large-cap focus.
  • Charges (OCF/TER): active funds cost more than passive, and open-ended funds more than closed-ended. Institutional share classes are cheaper.
  • Portfolio turnover ratio: more trading means more cost. Read it in context: satellites trade more, illiquid funds less, short bond funds more.
  • Administration, authorised vs unauthorised status, and onshore vs offshore.

Offshore funds and UK tax

Reporting fundReports its income to HMRC, and investors are taxed on it even if it isn't distributed. It is treated as interest if more than 60% is in cash or fixed interest, otherwise as dividends. Gains are subject to CGT.
Non-reporting fundIncome rolls up gross inside the fund. On disposal, the whole gain is taxed as income, so the CGT allowance can't be used.

Assessment of value

Fund managers or boards publish an annual assessment of value report. It asks whether charges are justified, looking at performance against the benchmark, quality of service, cost-effectiveness and overall value for money.

Check: what is survivorship bias?
Failed funds or companies drop out of performance studies, so the surviving average looks better than investors actually experienced.
Lesson 7

Structured products

A structured product packages a bond or deposit with derivatives to give a defined pay-off linked to an index or asset. Any protection is only as good as the issuer.

Common features: income or growth, not both; defined returns and risks; returns linked to an external measure (such as an index, gold or oil); a defined term; designed to be held to maturity; low secondary-market liquidity.

Structured depositsCash deposits from banks, with returns linked to an index. Designed to return at least the deposit, and covered by the FSCS.
Structured investmentsBuilt from derivatives and securities. Not FSCS-protected, and even "protected" capital depends on the issuer: counterparty risk.

Benefits: flexible, possible capital protection, tax efficiency, enhanced returns, risk reduction, diversification. Risks: inflexible once bought, capped returns, barrier breaches, inflation erosion, illiquidity, issuer credit risk, complexity, no shareholder rights (no dividends or votes), high upfront costs, and opaque pricing. Kick-out (autocall) plans mature early if the index is at or above a set level on an observation date.

TypeHow it works (book examples, $1,000 invested)
Principal-protectedCapital back at maturity plus a share of growth (for example 80% participation). Index +50% → $1,400. Index −50% → $1,000. Usually 5–7 years.
Capital-protected trackerCapital protection plus participation (for example 140% of DAX growth), in exchange for giving up dividends.
Buffer zoneNo loss until the index falls past the buffer (for example 20%), then 1% loss per 1% fall. Index −45% → lose 25% → $750. Usually 2–4 years.
SCaRPVariable protection with barriers and loss multipliers. A −20% barrier with 1.5:1: index −25% → lose 5 × 1.5 = 7.5% → $925.
Return-enhancedLeveraged upside (for example 2×) up to a cap (for example 26%), with full downside. Index +12% → +24%. Index +30% → capped at +26%. Index −12% → −12%. Usually 1–3 years.
Accelerated trackerFor example 200% of growth; if the index is down, you lose the same as the index. An accelerated income version is a "booster".
Reverse trackerRises when the underlying falls (a "bear certificate").
DigitalA fixed payment if a target is met at maturity, otherwise just capital back.

Listed structured products settle in CREST at T+2 and are treated as derivatives under FCA conduct rules (a two-way risk warning is required). They are usually designed so the pay-off is taxed as a capital gain, and can be held in a SIPP.

Check: a structured product with 100% capital protection is issued by a bank that later fails. Is the capital safe?
Not necessarily. For a structured investment the protection is only as strong as the issuer, and there is no FSCS cover. Only structured deposits have FSCS protection.
Lesson 8

ESG bonds and carbon as an asset class

ESG bonds work like normal bonds but must use the money raised for defined environmental or social purposes. Carbon has become a tradable asset through emissions markets.

BondProceeds used for
GreenProjects with environmental benefits, such as renewables and green buildings
BlueOcean and marine projects: biodiversity, wastewater, water pollution
Impact (impact debt)Prevention and early intervention for vulnerable groups
SocialSocial outcomes: basic infrastructure, health, education
SustainabilityA mix of green and social projects
Sustainability-linked (SLB)General purposes, but the bond's terms (such as the coupon) change depending on whether the issuer meets ESG targets
GenderReducing gender inequality and empowering women

ICMA's Green Bond Principles, Social Bond Principles, Sustainability Bond Guidelines and Sustainability-Linked Bond Principles require reporting and often third-party verification, which helps guard against greenwashing.

Carbon pricing

Carbon taxThe government sets a price per tonne emitted, but no cap on the amount.
Emissions trading (cap and trade)The government caps total emissions, issues permits (free or by auction) and lowers the cap each year. Firms trade surplus permits. Priced per tonne of CO₂e, through spot and derivatives.

The EU ETS (in force since 2005) is the largest, covering about 45% of EU emissions from over 11,000 installations. The UK ETS started in 2021. US schemes include RGGI and the Western Climate Initiative. Over 30 ETSs operate worldwide. ESG ratings (MSCI AAA–CCC, Sustainalytics, Moody's ESG) help, but vary widely between providers. More screening restrictions shrink the universe and may limit short-term performance.

Check: how does a sustainability-linked bond differ from a green bond?
A green bond's proceeds must fund green projects. An SLB's proceeds can be used for anything, but its terms change according to whether the issuer meets set ESG targets.
Lesson 9

Hedge funds, managed futures and cryptoassets

Alternatives promise returns that don't depend on the market, but bring leverage, illiquidity and, for crypto, almost no protection.

Hedge funds

The core idea is absolute return regardless of market direction: combine long and short positions to hedge out market risk and profit from selection skill. In practice, many focus more on geared returns.

  • Structure: US private partnerships, or unauthorised funds in offshore centres (Cayman, Dublin) with light regulation. A prime broker provides trading, credit and administration.
  • Full investment flexibility, use of gearing and derivatives.
  • Lock-in of one to three years, then dealing only monthly or quarterly. Minimums of $100,000 to $1m. Size is often capped to stay nimble.
  • Generally low correlation with equities and bonds, but correlation can jump in a shock.
  • Fees are often "2 and 20": 2% management plus 20% of performance above a hurdle (usually the risk-free rate), only after past losses are recovered. Managers are expected to invest their own money.
  • Risks: gearing, OTC settlement, currency, dealing delays, illiquidity, lack of visibility, fraud, and no independent trustees. Some are now launched as UCITS funds. Available as single funds or funds of hedge funds.

Managed futures funds use futures, often negatively correlated with a portfolio. The two common strategies are market-neutral (arbitrage) and trend-following.

Cryptoassets

Digital assets using distributed ledger technology (such as blockchain) with no middleman. Cryptocurrencies such as Bitcoin have no central bank; transactions are verified by "mining" and traded on online exchanges (Coinbase, Binance). Risks: extreme volatility, low liquidity, little regulation, no backing by assets or reserves, energy-hungry mining, and no compensation scheme. Some countries, including China, ban them. Meme coins (Dogecoin, Shiba Inu) are driven by trends and fear of missing out, with little utility.

NFTs are unique tokens on a blockchain (non-fungible: one can't be swapped for another), linked to a digital or physical item. They have no intrinsic value (it depends on what buyers will pay), and owning one doesn't give you the copyright or IP. Risks include fakes, sellers who don't own them, no regulation and no legal definition. The creator decides how scarce they are.

Check: what does "non-fungible" mean?
Each token is unique and can't be replaced by another one of the same value, unlike units of a cryptocurrency, which are interchangeable.
Lesson 10

Property

Property is unique, lumpy and slow to sell, which shapes how it is valued, held and benchmarked.

HeterogeneityEvery property is unique.
Subjective valuationNo central market or continuous prices.
IlliquiditySlow and costly to sell.
IndivisibilityBought whole, which limits diversification.
Finite supplyRestricted by planning rules, so demand drives prices.

Risks: property-specific (location, use, tenant quality, lease length, liquidity), market (interest rates, sector performance, rental growth) and vehicle (liquidity, diversification, gearing). Infrequent valuations make measures like standard deviation less reliable for property funds.

Ways to invest

  • Direct: rental income (often with upward-only rent reviews) plus capital growth, but highly illiquid.
  • Indirect: property company shares, REITs and funds. More liquid, but they move with the stock market.
  • Property unit trusts: can't be authorised, so they are unauthorised unit trusts.
  • PAIFs: must be an OEIC with at least 60% of net income from property investment. They pay rental income without corporation tax. Distributions are split into property income distributions (taxed as property income; charities, pensions and UK companies can receive them gross), interest and dividends.
  • LTAFs handle open-ended funds' liquidity mismatch. Closed-ended funds and funds holding property shares don't have that problem.
  • Property ETFs hold property shares and REITs, not buildings.

Yields

Gross rental yield
Annual rent ÷ Property value
Net rental yield
(Annual rent − Operating costs) ÷ Property value
Value £1.5m, rent £90,000, costs £15,000: gross 6%, net 75 ÷ 1,500 = 5%.

Valuation and sustainability standards

RICS Red BookRICS was founded in 1868. The Red Book contains mandatory standards for RICS valuers, plus advisory VPGAs. Its November 2024 update added ESG and aligned it with IVS.
IVSC: International Valuation StandardsGlobal standards used in about 100 countries: 7 General Standards and 8 Asset Standards.
AREFA trade association for real estate funds. Its Code of Practice covers governance, operation, dealing and reporting, at a minimum level and a best-practice level.
GRESBInvestor-led (founded 2009 by pension funds). It benchmarks the ESG performance of real estate and infrastructure, scored against all reporting entities and aligned with the UN SDGs and the Paris Agreement.
Check: which feature means you can't buy a tenth of an office block directly?
Indivisibility.
Lesson 11

Currencies, commodities and infrastructure

These are real assets and markets in their own right, each driven by different forces.

Foreign exchange

The world's largest market (about $6.6 trillion a day), very liquid, trading 24 hours a day five days a week. Currencies reflect each country's economy, interest rates and politics. Investors use FX for speculation and hedging (forwards, options, futures, swaps). Risks: exchange rate moves, volatility (even in developed currencies), patchy liquidity in some pairs, and hedging costs that can outweigh the benefit, especially in emerging markets.

Commodities

SoftsAgricultural products and livestock. Renewable and seasonal, perishable, weather-dependent and affected by geopolitics (Ukraine and grain in 2022). Hedged by farmers and processors.
HardsMetals and energy. Finite, capital-intensive to extract, demand tied to growth. Traded mainly on the LME, CME and Shanghai Futures Exchange. Gold is a safe haven and held by central banks.

Exchanges include CME Group (CBOT for agricultural products, CME for energy and metals), ICE, Euronext, the Dubai Mercantile Exchange and the LME. Most commodities are priced in US dollars, so non-dollar investors face currency risk. OPEC supply decisions drive energy prices. Investors use commodities to diversify and hedge inflation, usually through futures or funds.

Infrastructure

Roads, ports, hospitals and schools, plus digital infrastructure, renewables and water. Individuals invest through equity funds (infrastructure company shares: defensive, steady yield), debt funds (regular income, lower volatility) or direct funds (projects; mature projects are lower risk than early-stage ones). The attraction is higher yields than corporate bond funds with relatively low volatility, from long, often inflation-linked cash flows. Risks include market and economic cycles, government policy and liquidity. Closed-ended structures suit illiquid direct projects (often with gearing), while infrastructure ETFs have higher OCFs than mainstream ETFs.

Check: why is a closed-ended structure well suited to direct infrastructure?
Investors sell shares to each other, so the manager never has to sell illiquid projects at distressed prices to meet redemptions.
