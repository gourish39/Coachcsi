Lesson 1

The three financial statements and how they fit together

The balance sheet is a photograph, the income statement is a film of the year, and the cash flow statement is the bank statement.

Statement of financial position (balance sheet)What the company owns and owes on one day, the year-end. It tells you nothing about the day before or after.
Statement of comprehensive income (income statement)Revenue and costs over the whole year, ending in profit or loss. It answers "did we make money?"
Statement of cash flowsThe actual cash that came in and went out over the year. It answers "where did the cash go?"

Why profit and cash are different

The income statement uses accruals accounting: a sale counts when it is made, not when the customer pays. Sell £1,000 of goods on 60 days' credit on 31 December and you have £1,000 of revenue but no cash yet. Likewise, a £1m machine isn't charged in one go. It is spread over its life as depreciation, but the cash left the business on the day it was bought. That is why a profitable company can still run out of cash, and why the cash flow statement exists.

How the three link

  • Net income at the bottom of the income statement flows into retained earnings on the balance sheet, after any dividends.
  • The cash flow statement explains how the cash figure moved from last year's balance sheet to this year's.
  • The balance sheet always balances: Assets = Liabilities + Equity. Everything a company owns was paid for either by someone it owes (liabilities) or by its owners (equity).

Who must publish what

The bigger the company, the more it must disclose. Micro and small companies get exemptions (usually no audit). Medium-sized companies must add a cash flow statement and may need a short "special auditors' report". Listed plcs also publish a statement of changes in equity, an operating and financial review, governance statements and half-yearly reports. IFRS is principles-based (it sets out what good reporting looks like). US GAAP is rules-based (it tries to cover every case).

Check: can a company report a profit and have less cash at the end of the year?
Yes. If it sold a lot on credit (receivables went up), built up stock, or bought fixed assets, profit can be positive while cash falls. Profit is measured on an accruals basis; cash is not.
Check: which statement would you use to see a company's debts on 31 December?
The balance sheet, because it is a snapshot at one date.
Lesson 2

The balance sheet: what is owned, what is owed, and what belongs to shareholders

Assets minus liabilities is what belongs to the shareholders. Every line on the balance sheet is either something owned, something owed, or the owners' stake.

Assets: current vs non-current

Current assets turn into cash within a year in the normal course of business: stock, money owed by customers (receivables), short-term investments and cash. They are listed with the least liquid first and cash last. Non-current assets are kept to be used, not sold: buildings and machinery (tangible), patents, brands and purchased goodwill (intangible), and long-term stakes in other companies (investments).

Why stock is valued at the lower of cost and net realisable value

Accounting is deliberately prudent: don't overstate what you own. If stock that cost £100 can now only be sold for £70 after selling costs (its NRV), it goes on the balance sheet at £70. If it could be sold for £150, it stays at £100, because you don't count a profit before you've made it.

FIFO, LIFO and AVCO: a worked example

A shop buys 100 units at £1.00, then 100 more at £1.50, and sells 100 units at £2.00 each (£200 of sales).

MethodUnits treated as soldCost of salesProfitClosing stock
FIFO (first in, first out)The old £1.00 ones£100£100£150
AVCO (weighted average)Average £1.25£125£75£125
LIFO (last in, first out)The new £1.50 ones£150£50£100

When prices are rising, FIFO gives the highest profit and a closing stock value closest to current prices. LIFO gives the lowest profit and an out-of-date stock value, which is why IAS 2 bans it.

Depreciation and IAS 16

A machine bought for £100,000 that lasts 10 years helps earn revenue for all 10 years, so its cost is spread at roughly £10,000 a year. This is the matching idea: match costs to the revenue they help produce. Freehold land is not depreciated because it doesn't wear out. Under IAS 16 a company can keep property at cost, or revalue it to fair value. Any revaluation gain goes to a revaluation reserve, not to profit.

Liabilities: three kinds

Payables (creditors)Known amounts owed, such as supplier invoices and overdrafts.
AccrualsThe timing is known but the exact amount isn't yet, such as this quarter's electricity bill.
ProvisionsAn estimate of a liability from outside normal trading (such as a legal claim), or a reduction in an asset's value.

A proposed final dividend is not a liability at the year-end. The company doesn't owe it until shareholders approve it at the AGM.

Equity: share capital and reserves

When a company issues £1 nominal shares for £2.50, only the £1 goes to share capital; the extra £1.50 goes to share premium. Capital reserves (share premium, revaluation reserve, capital redemption reserve) are not profits the company has earned and received. They protect creditors as part of the capital base, so they cannot be paid out as dividends, although they can be turned into bonus shares. Revenue reserves (retained earnings) are earned profits kept in the business, and they can be paid out.

Common confusion: retained earnings are not a pile of cash. They are profits kept over the years, which may already have been spent on machinery, stock or anything else.

Off-balance sheet finance

If a company borrows through a separate vehicle (an SPV), the debt may not appear on its own balance sheet. Gearing then looks lower than it really is, and borrowing can be cheaper. Analysts look for it because it hides risk.

Check: a company writes down the value of its patents. Which part of the balance sheet falls?
Intangible non-current (fixed) assets.
Check: why can't the share premium account be used to pay dividends?
It is a capital reserve. It is money shareholders paid in, not profit the company earned, and it forms part of the capital base that protects creditors.
Lesson 3

The income statement: profit in layers

Each profit line takes away one more kind of cost, and each one tells you something different about the business.

Profit lineWhat has been taken awayWhat it tells you
Gross profitDirect costs of making the product (materials, labour, storage)How efficiently the company makes or buys what it sells
Operating profit (EBIT / PBIT)Running costs such as distribution and administrationHow well the business itself is run, before financing decisions
Profit before taxInterest paid, less interest and investment income receivedThe effect of how the company is financed
Net incomeTaxWhat is left for the shareholders: the "bottom line"

Exceptional items (for example a large one-off restructuring loss) are shown separately because they are unusual but material. Separating them lets you judge the underlying, repeatable profit. Other comprehensive income holds gains and losses not yet realised, such as revaluations. EBITDA also adds back depreciation and amortisation, which helps when comparing companies with very different asset bases or accounting rules.

EPS divides the profit belonging to ordinary shareholders (net income minus preference dividends) by the number of ordinary shares. It turns total profit into something you can compare per share.

Common confusion: dividends are not a cost of running the business. They are a share-out of profit to the owners, so they appear in the statement of changes in equity, not as an expense in the income statement.
Check: two companies have the same gross margin but very different operating margins. What does that tell you?
They make their products equally efficiently, but one has much higher running costs (distribution, admin, selling) than the other.
Lesson 4

The cash flow statement: following the money

It sorts every cash movement into running the business, investing in it, or financing it, and the pattern across the three tells you a lot.

OperatingCash from customers, minus cash paid to suppliers and staff, tax and interest. This is the core: can the business pay its own way?
InvestingBuying or selling fixed assets and investments, plus interest and dividends received.
FinancingRaising or repaying loans, issuing shares, paying dividends.

Why depreciation is added back

Depreciation reduced profit, but no cash left the business when it was charged; the cash left when the asset was bought. So to get from profit to cash, you add depreciation, amortisation and book losses back, and take book profits on disposals out.

Reading the pattern

HealthyOperating cash is positive and pays for investing in new assets, with some left for dividends or repaying debt.
WarningOperating cash is negative, and the gap is filled by selling assets (positive investing) and new borrowing (positive financing). This is the point of MCQ 72.

Long-lived assets should also be funded with long-term money. A 10-year machine paid for with a 1-year loan creates a refinancing problem.

Check: where does a share issue appear?
Financing activities, along with borrowing, repaying loans and paying dividends.
Lesson 5

Group accounts: influence vs control

The more say you have over another company, the more of it appears in your own accounts.

Little influence (usually under 20%)Just an investment. It is held at cost (less any impairment), and only the dividends you receive count as income.
Significant influence (20–50%): an associateYou don't control it, but you help steer it. You include your share of its profit as one line, and your share of its net assets as one line. This is the equity method.
Control (over 50%): a subsidiaryYou run it, so you add 100% of its assets, liabilities, revenue and costs to your own, then show the part owned by outsiders as minority interests.

Why dividends from an associate are left out

Under the equity method you have already counted your 30% share of the associate's profit. When that profit is paid to you as a dividend, counting it again would double-count it. So associate dividends do not enter the group income statement.

Why minority interests appear

If you own 75% of a subsidiary, you still add 100% of it to the group accounts because you control all of it. The 25% belonging to other shareholders is then shown separately: as a deduction at the bottom of the income statement and as a separate line in equity.

Common confusion: the 20% figure is only a guide. A 15% stake with a seat on the board and real influence is still an associate. Influence decides it, not the percentage alone.
Check: Company X owns 25% of Y and has significant influence. How is Y shown in X's accounts?
As an associate using the equity method: X's 25% share of Y's profits in the income statement, and its share of Y's net assets on the balance sheet.
Lesson 6

Profitability ratios: how well the money in the business is used

ROCE asks "what return does the business make on all the money invested in it?", and it can only rise through better margins or by selling more per pound of capital.

The four families of ratios

The workbook groups ratios into profitability (trading performance), liquidity (can it pay its bills?), investor ratios (returns to shareholders) and gearing (how risky its funding is). Before using a ratio, ask which question it answers.

ROCE and the "matching" rule

Capital employed is all the long-term money in the business: shareholders' funds plus borrowing (including the overdraft). Because it includes lenders' money, the profit you put on top must be the profit available to both lenders and shareholders, which is profit before interest (PBIT). Use the same principle everywhere: the profit on top must belong to the same people as the capital underneath.

ROCEPBIT ÷ (debt + equity). All providers of capital.
ROENet income ÷ equity. Shareholders only.

Two ways to make money

ROCE = PBIT margin × asset turnover. A supermarket earns a thin margin (say 3%) but turns its capital over many times a year. A jeweller earns a fat margin (say 30%) but sells slowly. Both can earn a similar ROCE. That is why the workbook says asset turnover and margin tend to move in opposite directions, and why you should compare a company with others in the same industry.

DuPont: where ROE comes from

ROE = net margin × asset turnover × equity multiplier. The first two measure how well the business is run. The equity multiplier (total assets ÷ equity) measures how much of the assets are funded by debt rather than by owners. A higher multiplier raises ROE without the business getting any better. That is why a rise in ROE driven only by the multiplier is a warning sign: it means more debt and more risk. Extended DuPont splits the margin further into operating margin, a tax effect and an interest effect, so you can see whether ROE moved because of operations, tax or financing.

What distorts ROCE

  • Raising money at the year-end: capital goes up but there hasn't been time to earn profit on it.
  • Revaluing assets upwards: capital goes up and the higher depreciation lowers profit, so ROCE falls twice.
  • Buying a subsidiary at the year-end: its capital is included but none of its profit is.
Check: ROCE is 18% and the company borrows at 6%. Should it be pleased?
Yes. Each pound of capital earns well above what it costs to borrow, so borrowing to expand should add value (as long as the risk stays manageable).
Check: Company A's ROE rose from 12% to 18%. Margin and asset turnover are unchanged. What happened?
Its equity multiplier rose, meaning it took on more debt relative to equity. The extra return is from leverage, and comes with extra risk.
Lesson 7

Debt and gearing: borrowing magnifies results both ways

Debt makes good years better and bad years worse for shareholders, because interest must be paid whatever happens.

A worked example (ignoring tax)

Two companies each have £100m of capital. A is funded entirely by equity. B has £50m of equity and £50m of debt at 6% (£3m of interest a year).

PBITA: ROE (no debt)B: ROE (50% debt)
Good year£10m10 ÷ 100 = 10%(10 − 3) ÷ 50 = 14%
Bad year£4m4 ÷ 100 = 4%(4 − 3) ÷ 50 = 2%

B's shareholders do better in good years and worse in bad years. Their earnings swing more. That is what investors mean when they say high gearing makes the earnings stream more volatile.

The two gearing measures

  • Debt-to-equity measures how the company is funded, from the balance sheet. Preference shares count as debt because their fixed dividend must be paid before ordinary shareholders get anything.
  • Interest cover (PBIT ÷ interest) measures how comfortably profit pays the interest, from the income statement. In the bad year above, B's cover is 4 ÷ 3 = 1.33×. That is below 1.5×, which the workbook treats as a warning sign.

Debt is attractive because it is usually cheaper than equity and the interest reduces tax. Too much of it, though, makes the company fragile, especially if rates rise on floating-rate debt.

Check: why is interest cover calculated with PBIT and not profit before tax?
Interest is paid out of profit before interest. Profit before tax has already had the interest taken off, so using it would understate the cover.
Lesson 8

Liquidity: can the company pay its bills on time?

Profitable companies still fail if cash isn't there when bills fall due. Liquidity ratios test that short-term safety.

Current ratio vs quick ratio

The current ratio compares everything that will turn into cash within a year with everything due within a year. But stock may not sell quickly, or may only sell at a discount, so the quick ratio (acid test) leaves stock out. It asks: if suppliers demanded payment tomorrow, could we pay without selling a single item? A current ratio of roughly 1.5–2 and a quick ratio above 1 are generally comfortable. A very high ratio isn't automatically good: it can mean idle cash or too much stock earning nothing.

The cash cycle as a timeline

A company buys stock on day 0 and pays the supplier on day 30. It sells the stock on day 60, and the customer pays on day 105.

Inventory days (stock sits on the shelf)60
+ Receivable days (waiting for the customer to pay)45
− Payable days (credit from the supplier)30
Cash cycle: days the company's own cash is tied up75

The company paid out on day 30 and got its money back on day 105: 75 days it has to fund itself. Shorter is better. It can shorten the cycle by holding less stock, collecting faster or paying suppliers later. Seasonal businesses can show misleading figures depending on the date of the balance sheet.

Z-score

The Z-score combines several ratios (gearing and sales mix among them) into one number that predicts the chance of failure within two years. Above 3 is safe, 1.8 to 3 is a grey area, below 1.8 means failure is likely, and a negative score means insolvency is imminent.

Check: a builder's merchant has a current ratio of 2.0 but a quick ratio of 0.6. What does that tell you?
Most of its current assets are stock. It looks safe on the current ratio, but it can only pay its short-term bills if it sells that stock.
Lesson 9

Cost of capital and economic value added

Capital isn't free: shareholders expect a return just as lenders charge interest, so a company only creates value when it earns more than its blended cost of capital.

WACC

A company is funded by a mix of equity and debt, and each has a cost. WACC blends them according to how much of each is used, at market values. Debt is cheaper for two reasons: lenders take less risk than shareholders (they are paid first), and interest reduces tax. That is why the cost of debt is multiplied by (1 − tax rate).

Equity is not free just because there's no interest bill. If shareholders don't get the return they expect, they sell and the share price falls. Too much equity means paying more for capital than necessary. Too much debt means taking on too much risk. There is no single "right" mix: capital-heavy industries such as car makers use more debt, while software companies use more equity.

WACC acts as the hurdle rate: a new project must earn more than WACC to be worthwhile.

EVA

Economic value added takes profit after tax and then subtracts a charge for all the capital used (invested capital × WACC). Ordinary profit only charges for debt (through interest); EVA also charges for equity. A positive EVA means the business earned more than its capital cost and created value. A company can report a profit and still have a negative EVA, which means it destroyed value.

Check: a company makes £5m of profit after tax on £100m of capital. Its WACC is 8%. Did it create value?
No. EVA = 5 − (100 × 8%) = −£3m. It earned 5% on capital that costs 8%.
Lesson 10

NPV and IRR: the time value of money

A pound today is worth more than a pound next year, so future cash has to be discounted before you can compare it with what you spend today.

Discounting

If you can earn 10% a year, £100 today becomes £110 in a year. Turning that round, £110 received in a year is worth £100 today: PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)n. The discount rate is the return you need, often the company's WACC.

NPV

Net present value = today's value of all the money coming in, minus what you spend. A positive NPV means the project beats the required return and adds value; accept it. A negative NPV means it falls short; reject it. The book's example: spend £75,000 to receive £20,000 a year for 5 years at 10%. The inflows are worth £75,815.74 today, so NPV = +£815.74.

IRR

The internal rate of return is the discount rate at which NPV is exactly zero: the return the project actually earns. If the IRR is above your required rate, the project is worth doing. It is found by trial and error, or estimated by interpolation: take a rate that gives a positive NPV and one that gives a negative NPV, and estimate where between them NPV crosses zero.

Common confusion: when choosing between projects, rank them by NPV, not IRR. A small project can have a high IRR but add little actual value. IRR is best used as the break-even rate.
Check: a project has an NPV of zero at 12%. What is its IRR?
12%. The IRR is, by definition, the rate that makes NPV zero.
Lesson 11

The limits of ratios, ESG disclosure, and economies of scale

Ratios raise questions rather than give answers, and what you can compare them with matters as much as the number itself.

Why ratios can mislead

Across industriesIndustries need very different amounts of capital (a utility vs a software firm), so "normal" ratios differ.
Within one industryCompanies may be at different stages. A young company may have high debt and weak cash flow but better long-term prospects.
Over timeThe data is historic. Accounting policies can change, and figures can be window-dressed or distorted by seasonality.

Countries that don't use IFRS add another layer of difference.

ESG disclosure: who does what

  • TCFD was set up in 2015 by the Financial Stability Board for consistent climate-risk disclosure. It was voluntary, but some countries made it mandatory. It disbanded in 2023, and the IFRS Foundation now monitors progress.
  • GRI is the most widely used standard. It covers a company's impact on the world, such as emissions, waste and biodiversity.
  • SASB is industry-specific. It focuses on the ESG issues that are financially material in each of about 80 industries. Its Materiality Map shows, for example, that emissions matter most in oil and gas, and water management in agriculture.
  • Marketing rules against greenwashing: the EU's SFDR requires standard ESG disclosures (comply or explain). The UK's SDR adds product labels and an anti-greenwashing rule that claims must be clear, fair and not misleading. The US has a Climate Disclosure Rule for material emissions.

Economies and diseconomies of scale

As a firm grows, its cost per unit falls through bulk buying, better use of equipment and spreading fixed costs. This continues down to the minimum efficient scale, the lowest point on the long-run average cost curve. Beyond it, diseconomies of scale set in: bureaucracy and coordination problems push unit costs back up. If the cost of one more unit (marginal cost) is below the average cost, the average is still falling.

Check: an investor wants to know which ESG issues matter financially for a bank. Which framework is designed for that?
SASB, using its industry-by-industry Materiality Map.

Worked example (the book's own pattern). Sales £5m, PBIT £1.5m, share capital £1m, reserves £5m, loans £1m, overdraft £0.5m.
Capital employed = 1 + 5 + 1 + 0.5 = £7.5m. ROCE = 1.5 ÷ 7.5 = 20%. Margin = 1.5 ÷ 5 = 30%. Asset turnover = 5 ÷ 7.5 = 0.67×. Check: 30% × 0.67 = 20%.

The method is right; only the decimals are printed wrong. The book also says net margin "can also be written as PBIT ÷ revenue". Use whichever profit figure the question names.

5. Balance sheet items and reserves Regular

  • Assets = Liabilities + Equity. Net assets = Shareholders' funds = Share capital + Reserves.
  • Current assets (stock, receivables, short-term investments, cash) are listed in order of liquidity and valued at the lower of cost and net realisable value.
  • Stock valuation when prices are rising: FIFO gives the highest profit. LIFO gives the lowest and is not allowed under IAS 2. AVCO is in between.
  • Non-current assets: tangible (property, plant and equipment), intangible (patents, brands, licences, purchased goodwill) and investments. Patents written down reduce intangible fixed assets (MCQ 36).
  • IAS 16: PPE can be held at cost (the cost model) or revalued periodically (the revaluation model).
  • Liabilities: payables are known amounts. Accruals have a known timing but an uncertain amount (the book's answer to MCQ 78). Provisions are estimates of liabilities from outside normal trading, or reductions in asset value.
  • Capital reserves (share premium, revaluation reserve, capital redemption reserve) cannot be paid as dividends, but can fund a bonus issue. Revenue reserves (retained earnings) can be paid out.
  • Share premium: 100m £1 shares issued at £2.50 adds £100m to share capital and £150m to share premium.
  • A proposed final dividend is not a liability at the year-end (IAS 10). It only becomes one once shareholders approve it.
  • Off-balance sheet finance (for example through SPVs) keeps debt off the balance sheet, so gearing looks lower than it really is.

7. Cash flow statement Regular

  • Operating: cash from customers, minus cash paid to suppliers and staff, tax and interest paid (in the book's example).
  • Investing: buying and selling fixed assets and investments, plus interest and dividends received.
  • Financing: dividends paid, borrowing raised or repaid, and shares issued.
  • Non-cash items such as depreciation, amortisation and book losses on disposals are added back. Book profits on disposals are deducted.
  • Why investors read it (MCQ 72): to see whether the company depends on selling fixed assets and borrowing to fund day-to-day operations. Positive operating cash flow is what matters.

8. WACC, EVA, NPV and IRR Regular

WACC
[E × Re + D × Rd × (1 − Tc)] ÷ (E + D)
Market values. The cost of debt is reduced for tax.
EVA
NOPAT − (Invested capital × WACC)
Invested capital = equity + long-term debt. Negative means value destroyed.
Present value
PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)^n
NPV = PV of inflows − PV of outflows
IRR by interpolation
r1 + [NPV+ ÷ (NPV+ − NPV−)] × (r2 − r1)
NPV− is negative, so the bottom line is effectively the sum of the two NPVs' sizes
EVA example (book). NOPAT $8,000. Equity $50,000 + debt $5,000 = $55,000 invested. WACC 9%. EVA = 8,000 − 4,950 = +$3,050, so value is created.
  • The cost of equity is usually higher than the cost of debt: shareholders take more risk and interest is tax-deductible.
  • WACC is the hurdle rate for projects and acquisitions.
  • Rank competing projects by NPV, not IRR. IRR is only the break-even rate where NPV = 0.

9–12. The shorter topics Occasional

  • Company size rules: micro and small companies are usually exempt from audit. Medium-sized companies must publish a cash flow statement and may need a "special auditors' report" (an abridged one). Large companies prepare full accounts. Listed plcs also include a statement of changes in equity, an operating and financial review, governance disclosures, and half-yearly (interim) reports, which are not fully audited. A company keeps its size status if it qualifies for two years in a row.
  • IFRS vs GAAP: IFRS is principles-based and GAAP is rules-based. The US still uses US GAAP. IFRS 18 applies from 1 January 2027.
  • Limitations of ratios: they use historic data, so they don't predict the future, and accounts can be window-dressed. Industries differ in how capital-intensive they are. Companies in the same industry can be at different stages (a young firm with high debt). The same company over time can change its accounting policies or be affected by seasonality. Countries use different accounting standards.
  • ESG disclosure: TCFD was set up by the FSB in 2015 and disbanded in 2023, with the IFRS Foundation taking over monitoring. GRI is the most widely adopted standard, focused on a company's impact. SASB is industry-specific (about 80 industries) and its Materiality Map shows which issues are financially material in each sector. SFDR is the EU's rule, on a comply-or-explain basis. SDR is the UK's version, with product labels and an anti-greenwashing rule. The US has a Climate Disclosure Rule.
  • Economies of scale: unit costs fall as output rises, down to the minimum efficient scale (MES). Beyond it, diseconomies of scale set in, such as management bureaucracy. If marginal cost is below average cost, average cost is falling.

Exam technique for Section 3

  • Write down the formula before you touch the numbers. Most wrong answers among the options come from the wrong denominator: total assets instead of capital employed, PBT instead of PBIT, or revenue instead of cost of sales.
  • Four-company tables: calculate all four. Don't guess from the biggest single input. A high margin can be outweighed by low asset turnover.
  • Check units: £000 vs £m, and days vs times.
  • Budget about 90 seconds a question. If a table question runs over 2 minutes, flag it and come back.
